### spleen
- latinName: Splen (Lien)
- summary: The largest secondary lymphoid organ, weighing 150 to 200 g (the "1 x 3 x 5 x 7 x 9 x 11" rule: 1 inch thick, 3 wide, 5 long, 7 ounces, lying under ribs 9 to 11). The red pulp (sinusoids and cords of Billroth) filters blood; the white pulp (periarteriolar lymphoid sheath of T cells, follicles of B cells, marginal zone) mounts immune responses. It develops from mesenchyme in the dorsal mesogastrium in the fifth week and is covered by peritoneum except at the hilum.
- location: In the left hypochondrium against ribs 9 to 11, its long axis along the tenth rib, behind the stomach, in front of the left kidney and above the splenic flexure. The gastrosplenic ligament carries the short gastric and left gastro-omental vessels; the splenorenal ligament carries the splenic vessels and the tail of the pancreas.
- function: Removes ageing and abnormal red cells and opsonised organisms, mounts antibody responses (especially against encapsulated bacteria), stores platelets (about one third) and has a fetal haematopoietic role. Sympathetic fibres from the coeliac plexus (T6 to T10) travel with the splenic artery and control the capsule and vessels.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the splenic artery from the coeliac trunk, tortuous along the upper border of the pancreas, dividing into terminal branches at the hilum. Venous: the splenic vein, which joins the superior mesenteric vein behind the neck of the pancreas to form the portal vein. Lymph: efferent vessels only, to pancreaticosplenic and then coeliac nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Fractures of the left lower ribs can rupture it, producing intraperitoneal bleeding and left shoulder tip pain (Kehr sign); it is the most commonly injured solid organ in blunt abdominal trauma. After splenectomy there is lifelong risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, Neisseria meningitidis), so vaccination and prophylaxis are needed; Howell-Jolly bodies appear on the blood film. Splenomegaly occurs in portal hypertension, malaria, infections, lymphoma and haemolytic anaemias. Sickle cell disease causes autosplenectomy. Accessory spleens are common.

### left-lobe-of-thymus
- latinName: Lobus sinister thymi
- summary: The left half of a bilobed primary lymphoid organ with a cortex of immature thymocytes and a medulla containing epithelial cells and Hassall corpuscles. The epithelium develops from the endoderm of the third pharyngeal pouch (with the inferior parathyroids); the lymphocytes originate from bone marrow. It is largest relative to body size in infancy, reaches its greatest weight (about 30 to 40 g) at puberty, then involutes and is replaced by fat.
- location: In the anterior superior mediastinum behind the manubrium and upper body of the sternum, in front of the left brachiocephalic vein, the aortic arch and its branches and the trachea, and in front of the upper pericardium. The cervical extension can reach the lower thyroid.
- function: Site of T cell maturation: positive selection (recognition of self MHC) and negative selection (deletion of self-reactive cells, helped by AIRE expression), which provides central tolerance. Autonomic fibres come from the vagus and cervical sympathetic ganglia.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: inferior thyroid, internal thoracic (thymic branches) and pericardiacophrenic arteries. Venous: left brachiocephalic, internal thoracic and inferior thyroid veins. Lymph: efferent vessels only, to parasternal, brachiocephalic and tracheobronchial nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Thymoma is associated with myasthenia gravis (about a third of patients), pure red cell aplasia and hypogammaglobulinaemia. DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion, third and fourth pouch defect) gives thymic hypoplasia with T cell deficiency, hypocalcaemia and cardiac outflow defects. Thymic masses are one of the "four Ts" of anterior mediastinal masses (thymoma, teratoma, thyroid, terrible lymphoma). In infants the thymus can form the "sail sign" on a chest radiograph.

### right-lobe-of-thymus
- latinName: Lobus dexter thymi
- summary: The right half of the thymus, with the same cortical and medullary structure, third pharyngeal pouch origin and pubertal involution as the left lobe.
- location: In the anterior superior mediastinum behind the sternum, in front of the superior vena cava, the right brachiocephalic vein, the great vessels and the right mediastinal pleura.
- function: T cell maturation and central tolerance, as for the left lobe. Innervation is from the vagus and sympathetic fibres, with small phrenic contributions.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: inferior thyroid, internal thoracic and pericardiacophrenic branches. Venous: mainly to the left brachiocephalic vein, with tributaries to the internal thoracic and inferior thyroid veins. Lymph: efferent only, to parasternal, brachiocephalic and tracheobronchial nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Thymoma and thymic hyperplasia (seen in myasthenia gravis) arise from either lobe; the right lobe is related to the superior vena cava, so large masses can cause superior vena cava obstruction. Thymectomy is performed for thymoma and in selected myasthenia patients.
