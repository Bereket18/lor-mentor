## Right kidney | Ren dexter  /  Left kidney | Ren sinister
Summary: One of two bean-shaped organs that filter the blood and produce urine. [Right: It usually sits slightly lower than the left because of the liver above it.] [Left: It usually sits slightly higher than the right.]
Location: Retroperitoneal, against the back wall of the abdomen beside the spine, roughly between the T12 and L3 vertebral levels. [Right: In front of it lie the liver, the descending part of the duodenum and the right colic flexure.] [Left: In front of it lie the stomach, spleen, pancreas and the left colic flexure.] The adrenal gland sits on its upper pole.
Function: Filters blood plasma to form urine and regulates fluid volume, electrolyte levels and acid-base balance. It also helps control blood pressure through renin, produces erythropoietin, and activates vitamin D.
Blood supply: [Right: Supplied by the right renal artery, a branch of the abdominal aorta, which divides into segmental arteries. Blood drains through the right renal vein into the inferior vena cava.] [Left: Supplied by the left renal artery, a branch of the abdominal aorta, which divides into segmental arteries. Blood drains through the left renal vein, which crosses in front of the aorta to reach the inferior vena cava.]
Clinical notes: Kidney stones, infection that ascends to the kidney (pyelonephritis) and chronic kidney disease are common. The lower ribs, surrounding fat and the renal fascia protect the kidney, but flank trauma can still injure it.

## Right ureter | Ureter dexter  /  Left ureter | Ureter sinister
Summary: A muscular tube, about 25 cm long, that carries urine from the kidney to the urinary bladder.
Location: Runs behind the peritoneum down the front of the psoas major muscle, crosses the pelvic brim near the bifurcation of the common iliac artery, then follows the pelvic side wall to enter the back of the bladder at an oblique angle. In males the ductus deferens crosses over it near the bladder. [Right: The abdominal part lies close to the inferior vena cava.] [Left: The pelvic part lies behind the sigmoid mesocolon.]
Function: Moves urine by waves of muscular contraction (peristalsis). Its oblique entry into the bladder acts as a one-way valve that limits backflow of urine.
Blood supply: Supplied in segments by nearby arteries: the renal artery (upper part), the gonadal artery or branches of the aorta and common iliac artery (middle part), and branches of the internal iliac artery such as the vesical arteries (lower part).
Clinical notes: Kidney stones tend to lodge at three narrow points: the junction with the renal pelvis, the pelvic brim, and where the ureter enters the bladder. Pain often radiates from the loin to the groin. The ureter's close relations in the pelvis put it at risk during pelvic and colorectal surgery.

## Urinary bladder | Vesica urinaria
Summary: A hollow muscular organ that stores urine and expels it during urination.
Location: In the pelvis behind the pubic symphysis, below the peritoneum when empty. It rises into the lower abdomen as it fills. In the male it lies in front of the rectum, with the prostate below its neck.
Function: Stores urine at low pressure and contracts its detrusor muscle to empty. Filling and emptying are coordinated by autonomic and somatic nerves.
Blood supply: Superior vesical arteries and, in males, inferior vesical arteries, from the internal iliac artery. Veins drain through the vesical venous plexus to the internal iliac veins.
Clinical notes: Urinary tract infection (cystitis), bladder stones, urinary retention and bladder cancer are important conditions. A full bladder rises above the pubic bone with the peritoneum lifted off its front wall, which allows suprapubic catheterization.

## Urethra | Urethra masculina
Summary: The tube that carries urine from the bladder to the outside. In males it also carries semen. This description applies to the male reference body.
Location: Runs from the bladder neck through the prostate (prostatic part), the pelvic floor (membranous part, surrounded by the external urethral sphincter) and the penis (spongy part), ending at the external urethral opening. It is about 18 to 20 cm long.
Function: Conducts urine during urination and semen during ejaculation. The internal and external sphincters help maintain continence.
Blood supply: The prostatic part is supplied by branches of the inferior vesical and middle rectal arteries; the membranous and spongy parts by branches of the internal pudendal artery.
Clinical notes: Urethral strictures, infection (urethritis) and obstruction from an enlarged prostate are common. Pelvic fractures can injure the membranous part, and straddle injuries can injure the bulbar part of the spongy urethra.

## Right renal artery | Arteria renalis dextra  /  Left renal artery | Arteria renalis sinistra
Summary: A large paired branch of the abdominal aorta that delivers blood to the kidney. [Right: It is usually longer than the left.] [Left: It is usually shorter than the right.]
Location: Arises from the abdominal aorta just below the origin of the superior mesenteric artery, at about the L1 to L2 level, and runs to the hilum of the kidney where it divides into segmental branches. [Right: It passes behind the inferior vena cava.] [Left: It passes behind the left renal vein.]
Function: Supplies the kidney with a large share of the cardiac output for filtration.
Blood supply: Vessel: see Location for origin and course.
Clinical notes: Renal artery stenosis can cause high blood pressure and kidney damage. Extra (accessory) renal arteries are a common variant that matters in transplant and vascular surgery.

## Right renal vein | Vena renalis dextra  /  Left renal vein | Vena renalis sinistra
Summary: A large vein that carries filtered blood from the kidney to the inferior vena cava. [Right: It is short.] [Left: It is longer than the right.]
Location: Leaves the hilum of the kidney in front of the renal artery and runs to the inferior vena cava. [Right: It passes almost directly into the inferior vena cava.] [Left: It crosses in front of the abdominal aorta, below the origin of the superior mesenteric artery, and receives the left gonadal vein and the left suprarenal vein.]
Function: Returns blood from the kidney to the systemic venous circulation.
Blood supply: Vessel: see Location for origin and course.
Clinical notes: Renal vein thrombosis can occur with some kidney diseases and cancers. [Right: The short right vein makes right-sided live donor nephrectomy technically harder.] [Left: It can be compressed between the superior mesenteric artery and the aorta (nutcracker phenomenon), and the entry of the left gonadal vein is relevant to left-sided varicocele.]

## Renal cortex | Cortex renalis
Summary: The outer layer of kidney tissue beneath the capsule.
Location: Lies just under the renal capsule and extends between the renal pyramids as the renal columns.
Function: Contains the renal corpuscles (glomeruli) and convoluted tubules, where blood is filtered and much of the filtrate is reabsorbed.
Blood supply: Interlobular (cortical radiate) arteries, branches of the arcuate arteries.
Clinical notes: Cortical thinning is seen in chronic kidney disease, and scarring can follow repeated infection. Renal cell carcinoma usually arises from tubule cells in the cortex.

## Renal medulla and pyramids | Medulla renalis, Pyramides renales
Summary: The inner part of the kidney, arranged as cone-shaped renal pyramids.
Location: Deep to the cortex; the base of each pyramid faces the cortex and its tip points toward a minor calyx.
Function: Contains the loops of Henle and collecting ducts that concentrate the urine.
Blood supply: Straight arterioles (vasa recta) arising from the arcuate arteries.
Clinical notes: Papillary necrosis and medullary sponge kidney affect this region.

## Renal papillae | Papillae renales
Summary: The tips of the renal pyramids, where urine leaves the kidney tissue.
Location: At the apex of each pyramid, projecting into a minor calyx.
Function: Collecting ducts open here and release urine into the minor calyx.
Blood supply: Small branches from the vasa recta and surrounding arterial branches.
Clinical notes: Papillary necrosis can damage the papillae in conditions such as diabetes, sickle cell disease and long-term painkiller misuse.

## Renal calyces | Calyces renales minores et majores
Summary: Cup-shaped channels that collect urine from the papillae and pass it on to the renal pelvis.
Location: In the renal sinus. Several minor calyces join to form major calyces, and the major calyces join to form the renal pelvis.
Function: Collect and funnel urine toward the renal pelvis.
Blood supply: Small branches of the renal artery within the renal sinus.
Clinical notes: Stones can form or lodge in the calyces. Blunted or dilated calyces on imaging can indicate scarring or obstruction (hydronephrosis).

## Renal pelvis | Pelvis renalis
Summary: A funnel-shaped space that collects urine from the major calyces and drains into the ureter.
Location: In the renal sinus, behind the renal vessels at the hilum, narrowing into the ureter at the pelviureteric junction.
Function: Collects urine from the calyces and passes it into the ureter.
Blood supply: Small branches of the renal artery.
Clinical notes: Obstruction at the pelviureteric junction can cause hydronephrosis. Stones and infection (pyelonephritis) commonly involve the renal pelvis.
