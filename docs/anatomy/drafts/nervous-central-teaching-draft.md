### cerebellum
- latinName: Cerebellum
- summary: About 10 percent of brain volume but more than half of its neurons (granule cells). Two hemispheres joined by the vermis, with anterior, posterior and flocculonodular lobes. The cortex has three layers (molecular, Purkinje, granular) and output passes through the deep nuclei (dentate, emboliform, globose, fastigial). It develops from the rhombic lips of the metencephalon.
- location: In the posterior cranial fossa below the tentorium cerebelli, behind the pons and medulla with the fourth ventricle between them. Three peduncles connect it to the brainstem: superior (midbrain), middle (pons) and inferior (medulla).
- function: Coordinates the timing, force and accuracy of movement, maintains posture and balance, and takes part in motor learning. Vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe): balance and eye movements. Spinocerebellum (vermis and intermediate zone): posture, gait and ongoing movement. Cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemisphere): planning, via the dentate nucleus and ventrolateral thalamus to motor cortex. Each hemisphere controls the ipsilateral body. Inputs: climbing fibres from the inferior olive and mossy fibres from the pons, spinal cord and vestibular nuclei.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: superior cerebellar artery and anterior inferior cerebellar artery (from the basilar artery) and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA, from the vertebral artery). Venous: superior and inferior cerebellar veins to the straight, transverse and petrosal sinuses.
- clinicalNotes: Cerebellar signs are ipsilateral: dysdiadochokinesia, ataxia, nystagmus, intention tremor, slurred speech and hypotonia (DANISH). Vermis lesions cause truncal ataxia (medulloblastoma in children); hemisphere lesions cause limb ataxia. PICA stroke and cerebellar haemorrhage can swell and compress the fourth ventricle, causing hydrocephalus and tonsillar herniation. Other causes include alcohol, paraneoplastic degeneration, haemangioblastoma (von Hippel-Lindau), otogenic abscess and Chiari I malformation.

### pons
- latinName: Pons
- summary: The middle part of the brainstem. The basal pons contains pontine nuclei, corticopontine, corticospinal and transverse pontocerebellar fibres; the tegmentum contains the reticular formation and the nuclei of cranial nerves V to VIII. It develops from the metencephalon.
- location: Between the midbrain and the medulla, in front of the fourth ventricle and cerebellum and behind the clivus. The basilar artery runs in the basilar groove. Cranial nerve V emerges from the mid-pons; VI, VII and VIII emerge at the pontomedullary junction.
- function: Relays signals from the cerebral cortex to the cerebellum, contains respiratory modulating centres (pneumotaxic and apneustic), controls horizontal gaze (paramedian pontine reticular formation, PPRF) and contains the motor nuclei for chewing and facial expression and the REM sleep generators.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: paramedian and circumferential branches of the basilar artery, with the anterior inferior and superior cerebellar arteries. Venous: pontine veins to the petrosal and basal veins.
- clinicalNotes: Basilar artery occlusion can cause locked-in syndrome (quadriplegia and loss of speech with preserved vertical eye movement and blinking). Ventral pontine lesions cause Millard-Gubler syndrome (ipsilateral VI and VII palsy, contralateral hemiparesis). PPRF lesions cause ipsilateral gaze palsy; MLF lesions cause internuclear ophthalmoplegia (multiple sclerosis). Pontine haemorrhage causes pinpoint pupils and coma. Central pontine myelinolysis follows over-rapid sodium correction. Diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma affects children.

### midbrain
- latinName: Mesencephalon
- summary: The upper brainstem, with the tectum (superior and inferior colliculi), the tegmentum (red nucleus, substantia nigra, periaqueductal grey, nuclei of III and IV) and the cerebral peduncles (crus cerebri). The cerebral aqueduct runs through it. It develops from the mesencephalon.
- location: Between the pons and the diencephalon, passing through the tentorial notch. Cranial nerve III emerges in the interpeduncular fossa; IV emerges dorsally below the inferior colliculus, the only cranial nerve to do so.
- function: Superior colliculi direct saccades and orienting movements and the pretectum mediates the pupillary light reflex; inferior colliculi relay auditory pathways; the substantia nigra (pars compacta, dopamine) supplies the striatum; the Edinger-Westphal nucleus provides parasympathetic supply for pupil constriction and accommodation; the periaqueductal grey modulates pain and defensive behaviour.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: P1 and P2 segments of the posterior cerebral artery, quadrigeminal, superior cerebellar, posterior choroidal and basilar perforators. Venous: basal vein of Rosenthal and great cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Weber syndrome (ipsilateral III palsy with contralateral hemiparesis), Benedikt syndrome (with contralateral tremor from red nucleus involvement) and Claude syndrome. Loss of nigral neurons causes Parkinson disease. Parinaud syndrome (impaired upgaze, convergence-retraction nystagmus, light-near dissociation) follows pineal or tectal compression. Uncal herniation compresses the oculomotor nerve, causing a dilated pupil and ptosis, and midbrain haemorrhages (Duret). Aqueductal stenosis and top of the basilar syndrome occur.

### medulla-oblongata
- latinName: Medulla oblongata
- summary: The lowest part of the brainstem, about 3 cm long, with pyramids (corticospinal tracts), olives (inferior olivary nuclei) and the inferior cerebellar peduncles. Nuclei include the gracilis and cuneatus, the nucleus ambiguus, solitary nucleus, dorsal vagal nucleus, hypoglossal nucleus and spinal trigeminal nucleus. It develops from the myelencephalon.
- location: From the pontomedullary junction to the foramen magnum (the decussation of the pyramids at about C1), in front of the cerebellum and fourth ventricle and behind the clivus. Cranial nerves IX, X, XI (cranial root) and XII emerge from it.
- function: Houses the cardiovascular and respiratory control centres (dorsal and ventral respiratory groups), and the reflexes of swallowing, coughing, vomiting and sneezing. It relays the dorsal column pathway (medial lemniscus) and contains the pyramidal decussation. Motor decussation means each hemisphere controls the opposite limbs.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vertebral arteries, anterior spinal artery (medial medulla), posterior inferior cerebellar artery (lateral medulla) and posterior spinal arteries. Venous: medullary veins to the spinal and cerebellar veins.
- clinicalNotes: Medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome (anterior spinal artery): contralateral hemiparesis and loss of proprioception, ipsilateral tongue deviation. Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome (PICA or vertebral): ipsilateral facial and contralateral body loss of pain and temperature, Horner syndrome, hoarseness, dysphagia, vertigo and ataxia. Raised intracranial pressure with tonsillar herniation compresses the medulla (Cushing reflex: hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing). Chiari malformation, syringobulbia and foramen magnum tumours also involve it. Brainstem reflexes are tested in brainstem death assessment.

### fourth-ventricle
- latinName: Ventriculus quartus
- summary: A diamond-shaped CSF space. Its floor is the rhomboid fossa (facial colliculus, hypoglossal and vagal trigones, obex, striae medullares, sulcus limitans) and its roof is formed by the superior medullary velum and the inferior medullary velum with choroid plexus. It develops from the cavity of the rhombencephalon.
- location: Behind the pons and upper medulla, in front of the cerebellum. It continues with the cerebral aqueduct above and the central canal below, and CSF leaves through the median aperture (Magendie) and two lateral apertures (Luschka) into the subarachnoid space, via the cisterna magna and cerebellopontine angles.
- function: Produces CSF through its choroid plexus and conducts CSF from the ventricular system to the subarachnoid space. Its floor carries the nuclei of cranial nerves V to XII. The area postrema (chemoreceptor trigger zone) lies in its floor.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: posterior inferior cerebellar artery for the choroid plexus, with anterior inferior cerebellar artery branches. Venous: cerebellar and medullary veins.
- clinicalNotes: Outlet obstruction causes hydrocephalus; posterior fossa tumours such as medulloblastoma, ependymoma and pilocytic astrocytoma in children, Dandy-Walker malformation and Chiari II malformation are important causes. A lesion at the facial colliculus affects the facial nerve fibres looping around the abducens nucleus, giving ipsilateral facial palsy and gaze palsy. Area postrema irritation causes vomiting.

### third-ventricle
- latinName: Ventriculus tertius
- summary: A narrow midline slit between the two thalami. Its lateral walls are the thalamus above and the hypothalamus below the hypothalamic sulcus; the interthalamic adhesion crosses it. The anterior wall is the lamina terminalis, anterior commissure and columns of the fornix; the floor includes the optic chiasm, infundibulum, tuber cinereum and mammillary bodies; the roof is the tela choroidea with choroid plexus. It is the cavity of the diencephalon.
- location: In the midline of the diencephalon. It communicates with the lateral ventricles through the interventricular foramina (of Monro) anteriorly and with the fourth ventricle through the cerebral aqueduct posteriorly. Recesses: optic, infundibular, suprapineal and pineal.
- function: Produces CSF in its choroid plexus and conducts CSF between the lateral ventricles and the aqueduct. It forms the medial boundary of the thalami and hypothalamus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: medial posterior choroidal arteries (from the posterior cerebral artery) to the choroid plexus. Venous: internal cerebral veins drain into the great cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Colloid cysts at the interventricular foramen cause obstructive hydrocephalus, with positional headache and sudden death. Craniopharyngioma, suprasellar extension of a pituitary macroadenoma and pineal tumours can obstruct or compress it. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy bypasses obstruction at or below the aqueduct. Periventricular lesions (mammillary bodies) occur in Wernicke encephalopathy.

### central-canal-of-spinal-cord
- latinName: Canalis centralis
- summary: A tiny ependyma-lined channel (0.1 to 0.2 mm) through the grey commissure of the spinal cord, the remnant of the neural tube lumen. It is often partly obliterated in adults.
- location: In the central grey commissure between the anterior and posterior grey commissures. Superiorly it continues with the fourth ventricle at the obex (closed medulla); inferiorly it widens into the terminal ventricle in the conus medullaris at about L1.
- function: Carries a very small volume of CSF. Ependymal cells lining the canal act as a niche for neural progenitor cells.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: sulcal (central) branches of the anterior spinal artery supply the central grey matter around the canal. Venous: central and sulcal veins draining to the radicular veins.
- clinicalNotes: Syringomyelia is cystic cavitation expanding from the canal, classically in the cervical cord with Chiari I malformation: a cape-like loss of pain and temperature (crossing spinothalamic fibres in the anterior white commissure) with preserved touch, and lower motor neuron weakness of the hands. Central cord syndrome follows hyperextension injury, with arms weaker than legs. Ependymoma arises from the canal lining.

### cerebral-aqueduct
- latinName: Aqueductus cerebri (Sylvii)
- summary: A narrow channel about 1.5 cm long and 1 to 2 mm wide, lined by ependyma and surrounded by periaqueductal grey matter.
- location: Through the midbrain, between the tectum (dorsal) and the tegmentum (ventral), at the level of the superior and inferior colliculi. It joins the third and fourth ventricles.
- function: Conducts CSF from the third to the fourth ventricle. The surrounding periaqueductal grey modulates pain and defensive responses.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: perforating branches of the posterior cerebral, quadrigeminal and superior cerebellar arteries. Venous: basal vein and great cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Aqueductal stenosis is a common cause of obstructive hydrocephalus, causing enlargement of the lateral and third ventricles with a normal-sized fourth ventricle. Causes: congenital (including X-linked L1CAM mutations), tumour, haemorrhage and infection. Upgaze palsy (Parinaud syndrome) follows compression of the dorsal midbrain. Treatment: endoscopic third ventriculostomy or shunting.

### hypothalamus
- latinName: Hypothalamus
- summary: A small (about 4 g) diencephalic region below the hypothalamic sulcus, divided into preoptic, supraoptic (anterior), tuberal and mammillary (posterior) regions. Key nuclei: suprachiasmatic, supraoptic, paraventricular, arcuate, ventromedial, dorsomedial, lateral hypothalamic area and mammillary bodies.
- location: Below the thalamus, forming the floor and lower walls of the third ventricle. Anterior: lamina terminalis and optic chiasm. Posterior: mammillary bodies. Inferior: the infundibulum and pituitary gland.
- function: Maintains homeostasis. Temperature (anterior cools, posterior warms), osmoregulation and thirst (osmoreceptors and ADH), hunger (lateral) and satiety (ventromedial, arcuate: leptin), circadian rhythm (suprachiasmatic nucleus), sleep, stress (CRH), reproduction (GnRH) and autonomic control through descending pathways. Releasing and inhibiting hormones reach the anterior pituitary through the portal system; ADH and oxytocin pass to the posterior pituitary. Connections: medial forebrain bundle, fornix and mammillothalamic tract, with limbic and brainstem afferents.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: branches of the circle of Willis (anterior communicating, posterior communicating and P1 segments) and the superior hypophyseal arteries. Venous: to the cavernous sinus and basal veins.
- clinicalNotes: Central diabetes insipidus, SIADH, hypothalamic obesity (Prader-Willi syndrome) and Kallmann syndrome are important. Craniopharyngioma, pituitary macroadenoma and hamartoma compress it. Thiamine deficiency damages the mammillary bodies in Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. Loss of lateral hypothalamic orexin neurons causes narcolepsy. Hypothalamic hamartoma causes gelastic seizures and precocious puberty.

### tuber-cinereum
- latinName: Tuber cinereum
- summary: A grey, slightly convex area of the hypothalamus between the mammillary bodies (behind) and the optic chiasm (in front), tapering to the infundibulum and its median eminence. It contains the arcuate (infundibular) and ventromedial nuclei and other tuberal nuclei.
- location: On the base of the brain, forming part of the floor of the third ventricle, in the interpeduncular and chiasmatic cisterns.
- function: Neurons of the median eminence release hypophysiotropic hormones into the primary capillary plexus of the pituitary portal system. The arcuate nucleus regulates appetite (NPY and POMC neurons), pulsatile GnRH release and dopamine-mediated inhibition of prolactin.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: superior hypophyseal arteries (internal carotid) and branches of the posterior communicating artery. Venous: portal veins to the anterior pituitary and the cavernous sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Hypothalamic hamartoma of the tuber cinereum causes gelastic (laughing) seizures and central precocious puberty. Craniopharyngioma and optic pathway glioma arise nearby. Stalk transection raises prolactin. Lesions here are linked with hyperphagia and obesity.

### pineal-body
- latinName: Corpus pineale (Glandula pinealis)
- summary: A small (about 8 x 5 mm, 100 to 150 mg) gland of pinealocytes and glial cells, with calcified deposits (corpora arenacea, "brain sand") that increase with age. It is an epithalamic structure derived from the roof of the diencephalon.
- location: In the midline, attached by the pineal stalk to the posterior wall of the third ventricle, above the superior colliculi and below the splenium of the corpus callosum, in the quadrigeminal cistern near the great cerebral vein. The habenular and posterior commissures lie at its stalk.
- function: Secretes melatonin, mainly at night, controlling circadian and seasonal rhythms. Light information from the retina passes to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, then through the spinal cord to the superior cervical ganglion, whose postganglionic sympathetic fibres reach the gland.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: medial posterior choroidal arteries (posterior cerebral). Venous: internal cerebral veins and the great cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Calcification is a midline landmark on skull imaging, and shift of a calcified pineal indicates a mass effect. Pineal region tumours (germinoma most commonly, pineocytoma, pineoblastoma, teratoma) cause Parinaud syndrome and obstructive hydrocephalus; germinomas can secrete beta-hCG. Pineal cysts are usually incidental. Melatonin is used for jet lag and sleep disorders.

### habenula
- latinName: Habenula
- summary: Paired small nuclei (medial and lateral habenular nuclei) of the epithalamus, joined by the habenular commissure, forming the habenular trigone.
- location: At the dorsomedial posterior thalamus, in front of the pineal body, beside the third ventricle.
- function: Receives limbic input (septal nuclei, lateral hypothalamus, globus pallidus internus) through the stria medullaris thalami and projects through the fasciculus retroflexus to the interpeduncular nucleus, ventral tegmental area and raphe nuclei. It encodes aversion and negative reward prediction, and influences mood, sleep and circadian rhythm.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: medial posterior choroidal arteries. Venous: internal cerebral veins.
- clinicalNotes: Habenular hyperactivity has been linked to depression, and deep brain stimulation of the habenula has been tested in treatment-resistant depression (experimental). Calcification of the habenular commissure is a normal imaging landmark.
