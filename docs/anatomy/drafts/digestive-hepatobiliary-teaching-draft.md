# MD-level teaching text: digestive-hepatobiliary (48 structures)

**Status: AI-drafted, pending faculty review.** Original wording, written from general anatomical knowledge, not copied from any textbook and not yet verified by faculty. Load as `isReviewed=false`.

Field use: summary (structure, histology, embryology); location (levels and relations); function (physiology and innervation); bloodSupply (artery, vein, lymph); clinicalNotes (pathology, imaging, procedures). British spelling.

Segment naming used by the 3D model and mapped to Couinaud numbering (reviewer to confirm each against the model): anterior superior = VIII, anterior inferior = V, posterior superior = VII, posterior inferior = VI (right liver); lateral superior = II, lateral inferior = III, medial superior = IVa, medial inferior = IVb (left liver); caudate = I.

Common facts: the portal triad (portal vein branch, hepatic artery branch and bile duct in a Glissonian sheath) runs through the centre of each segment, whereas the hepatic veins run between sections and segments. The liver receives about 75 percent of its blood from the portal vein and 25 percent from the hepatic artery; the bile ducts depend on the hepatic artery alone (peribiliary plexus). Autonomic supply to the liver and biliary tree: hepatic plexus (sympathetic T7 to T10, vagus). Lymph: hepatic nodes at the porta hepatis then coeliac nodes.

### trunk-of-right-portal-vein
- latinName: Truncus venae portae dextrae
- summary: The short, wide main stem of the right branch of the portal vein before it divides into anterior and posterior sectoral branches. The portal vein itself (about 8 cm, 1.2 cm wide) forms behind the neck of the pancreas from the superior mesenteric and splenic veins at L1 to L2 and divides at the porta hepatis.
- location: Enters the right lobe at the porta hepatis, 2 to 3 cm long, behind the right hepatic artery and in front of the caudate process.
- function: Carries portal blood to the right liver (segments V to VIII). Nerve supply: the hepatic plexus around the pedicle.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vasa vasorum from hepatic artery branches. Venous: it is a vein, and the right liver drains through the hepatic veins. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Portal vein embolisation of the right trunk makes the left lateral section hypertrophy before right hepatectomy. Right portal vein thrombosis causes right lobe atrophy with compensatory left lobe and caudate hypertrophy. It is the usual route for the portal limb of a TIPS.

### trunk-of-left-portal-vein
- latinName: Truncus venae portae sinistrae
- summary: The main stem of the left branch of the portal vein, longer and narrower than the right (about 4 to 6 cm), with a transverse part and an umbilical part that turns forward in the umbilical fissure.
- location: Runs along the base of segment IV in the porta hepatis; the ligamentum venosum attaches to it, and the ligamentum teres (remnant of the umbilical vein) joins at the umbilical portion.
- function: Carries portal blood to the left lateral and medial sections (segments II, III and IV) and to the caudate lobe by small branches.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vasa vasorum from hepatic artery branches. Venous: it is a vein. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: In portal hypertension the umbilical vein can recanalise, producing a caput medusae. The umbilical portion is the landmark in left lateral sectionectomy and for the plane to segments II and III. Hilar cholangiocarcinoma often encases it, determining resectability.

### right-portal-vein
- latinName: Vena portae dextra
- summary: The right portal vein with its anterior sectoral branch (to segments V and VIII) and posterior sectoral branch (to segments VI and VII).
- location: In the right lobe of the liver, starting at the porta hepatis and dividing inside the right liver.
- function: Delivers portal blood from the gut to the right liver. Nerve supply: hepatic plexus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vasa vasorum from hepatic artery branches. Venous: it is a vein. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Portal vein thrombosis (cirrhosis, thrombophilia, malignancy, abdominal infection) can involve it and causes cavernous transformation. Living-donor right lobe transplants require careful division of the right portal pedicle. Imaging of its branching pattern guides segmental resection.

### left-portal-vein
- latinName: Vena portae sinistra
- summary: The left portal vein with its lateral branches (to segments II and III) and medial branches (to segments IVa and IVb), and small caudate branches.
- location: In the left liver, starting at the porta hepatis, with the umbilical portion in the umbilical fissure.
- function: Delivers portal blood to the left lobe. Nerve supply: hepatic plexus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vasa vasorum from hepatic artery branches. Venous: it is a vein. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Variants of the left portal vein branching matter in left hepatectomy and in living-donor left lobe transplantation. Segment IV branches arise from the umbilical portion and can be injured in left lateral sectionectomy.

### anterior-superior-segmental-branch-of-right-portal-vein
- latinName: Ramus segmenti VIII venae portae dextrae
- summary: The portal vein branch that supplies segment VIII (anterior superior). Portal veins are thin-walled, valveless vessels that carry nutrient-rich, partly deoxygenated blood from the gut, spleen and pancreas to the hepatic sinusoids, running in the portal triad with the artery and bile duct.
- location: Inside the right anterior section, in the upper anterior part of the right liver directly beneath the diaphragm, branching from the right portal vein.
- function: Delivers about three quarters of the blood to segment VIII for metabolism, detoxification and storage of absorbed nutrients. Nerve supply is by the hepatic plexus accompanying the pedicle.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the walls are supplied by small hepatic artery branches (vasa vasorum). Venous: it is itself a vein; the segment drains into the hepatic veins. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Portal hypertension (portal pressure above about 10 mmHg or HVPG above 5 mmHg, usually cirrhosis) causes varices, ascites and splenomegaly; portal vein thrombosis causes segmental perfusion defects. Portal vein embolisation is used to enlarge the future liver remnant before major resection. Its subdiaphragmatic position makes ablation and resection difficult and exposes it to hydatid cysts and amoebic abscesses that can rupture into the chest.

### caudate-lobe-branch-of-right-portal-vein
- latinName: Ramus lobi caudati venae portae dextrae
- summary: A small branch of the right portal vein to the caudate lobe (segment I).
- location: Arises near the portal bifurcation and runs backward to the caudate process and lobe.
- function: Supplies the caudate lobe with portal blood, one of its several portal inputs.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: vasa vasorum. Venous: it is a vein; the caudate drains directly to the inferior vena cava by short hepatic veins. Lymph: hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Dual left and right portal inputs let the caudate lobe hypertrophy in cirrhosis and Budd-Chiari syndrome, and these small branches are divided in caudate lobectomy.

### right-hepatic-vein
- latinName: Vena hepatica dextra
- summary: The largest of the three main hepatic veins, draining the right posterior section (segments VI and VII) and part of the right anterior section. Hepatic veins lack valves, have thin walls and run in intersectional planes, unlike the portal triads.
- location: In the right intersectional (right portal) fissure, entering the inferior vena cava just below the diaphragm at about T8.
- function: Returns blood from the hepatic sinusoids to the inferior vena cava (about 1.5 litres per minute for the liver as a whole).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none of its own; it carries post-hepatic blood. Venous: it is a vein, draining into the inferior vena cava. Lymph: hepatic lymphatics follow the veins to diaphragmatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Hepatic vein thrombosis causes Budd-Chiari syndrome (abdominal pain, ascites, hepatomegaly; causes include myeloproliferative disease, oral contraceptives and paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria). Congestion from right heart failure produces a nutmeg liver. An accessory inferior right hepatic vein (10 to 15 percent) allows segment VI drainage after right hepatectomy. TIPS is placed between a hepatic vein and the portal vein.

### left-hepatic-vein
- latinName: Vena hepatica sinistra
- summary: A hepatic vein that drains the left lateral section (segments II and III) and often part of segment IVa; it commonly joins the middle hepatic vein before entering the inferior vena cava.
- location: In the left intersectional fissure (the umbilical fissure plane), joining the inferior vena cava just below the diaphragm.
- function: Returns blood from the left lateral liver to the inferior vena cava.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none of its own. Venous: it is a vein, draining into the inferior vena cava. Lymph: hepatic lymphatics to diaphragmatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Its course marks the plane between segments II and III in left lateral sectionectomy. Thrombosis or compression (tumour, hepatomegaly) causes left-sided hepatic congestion; hepatic vein pressure gradient measurements use a hepatic vein catheter.

### anterior-inferior-segmental-tributary-of-middle-hepatic-vein
- latinName: Tributaria segmenti V venae hepaticae mediae
- summary: A tributary of the middle hepatic vein, which runs in the main portal fissure along the Cantlie line and drains segments IV, V and VIII, that drains segment V (anterior inferior).
- location: In the lower part of the main portal fissure on the right of the gallbladder fossa.
- function: Returns blood from segment V to the middle hepatic vein and then the inferior vena cava.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none of its own. Venous: it is a vein, draining into the middle hepatic vein. Lymph: hepatic lymphatics.
- clinicalNotes: Large segment V and VIII tributaries can be preserved or reconstructed in right hepatectomy and in living-donor right lobe transplants (middle hepatic vein reconstruction) to prevent congestion of the remnant.

### hepatic-artery-proper
- latinName: Arteria hepatica propria
- summary: The artery supplying the liver, gallbladder and parts of the stomach and duodenum, about 4 cm long, arising from the common hepatic artery (coeliac trunk) after the gastroduodenal artery, giving the right gastric artery and dividing into right and left hepatic arteries.
- location: Ascends in the free edge of the lesser omentum (hepatoduodenal ligament) in front of the portal vein and to the left of the common bile duct, in the portal triad.
- function: Supplies about a quarter of liver blood flow and half of its oxygen, and all the oxygen to the bile ducts. Sympathetic nerves from the coeliac plexus control its tone.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: it is an artery. Venous: the liver drains by hepatic veins. Lymph: hepatic nodes in the hepatoduodenal ligament.
- clinicalNotes: Variants are common (replaced right hepatic artery from the superior mesenteric artery in 10 to 15 percent; replaced left from the left gastric artery) and matter in pancreaticoduodenectomy and liver transplantation. The Pringle manoeuvre clamps this ligament to control bleeding. Thrombosis after transplant causes biliary necrosis; injury occurs in cholecystectomy and hepatic artery infusion pumps are placed here.

### trunk-of-right-hepatic-artery
- latinName: Truncus arteriae hepaticae dextrae
- summary: The right branch of the proper hepatic artery, from which the cystic artery usually arises, dividing into anterior and posterior sectoral branches.
- location: Passes behind the common hepatic duct to enter the right liver, within Calot triangle (cystic duct, common hepatic duct and the liver edge) where it lies close to the cystic artery.
- function: Supplies the right liver, the gallbladder and the right hepatic duct. Sympathetic control through the hepatic plexus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: it is an artery. Venous: hepatic veins and cystic vein. Lymph: hepatic and cystic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: In laparoscopic cholecystectomy the right hepatic artery (sometimes tortuous, a caterpillar-hump) may be mistaken for the cystic artery; the critical view of safety avoids injury. Aberrant right hepatic arteries from the superior mesenteric artery run behind the portal vein.

### anterior-superior-segmental-hepatic-artery
- latinName: Arteria segmenti VIII
- summary: The branch of the right hepatic artery that supplies segment VIII (anterior superior), running in the portal triad. It supplies the segment with oxygenated blood and, through the peribiliary plexus, its bile ducts.
- location: In the portal pedicle of segment VIII, within the right anterior section, in the upper anterior part of the right liver directly beneath the diaphragm.
- function: Provides about a quarter of the segment's blood flow but about half of its oxygen, and the sole blood supply of the intrahepatic bile ducts. The vessel is under sympathetic (vasoconstrictor) control through the hepatic plexus; blood flow is buffered by the hepatic arterial buffer response when portal flow falls.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: it is an artery; the wall is nourished by vasa vasorum. Venous drainage of the segment: the hepatic veins. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Hepatocellular carcinoma is hypervascular in the arterial phase, so transarterial chemoembolisation is delivered through segmental branches of this kind. Hepatic artery thrombosis (the most feared early complication of liver transplantation) causes bile duct ischaemia and strictures. Aneurysm or injury can follow percutaneous procedures and cholecystectomy. Its subdiaphragmatic position makes ablation and resection difficult and exposes it to hydatid cysts and amoebic abscesses that can rupture into the chest.

Aneurysm or injury can follow percutaneous procedures and cholecystectomy. Hilar tumours often involve the pedicles of segment IV.

### medial-inferior-segmental-hepatic-artery
- latinName: Arteria segmenti IVb
- summary: The branch of the left hepatic artery that supplies segment IVb (medial inferior), running in the portal triad. It supplies the segment with oxygenated blood and, through the peribiliary plexus, its bile ducts.
- location: In the portal pedicle of segment IVb, within the left medial section, in the lower part of the medial segment (the quadrate lobe), in front of the porta hepatis and above the gallbladder.
- function: Provides about a quarter of the segment's blood flow but about half of its oxygen, and the sole blood supply of the intrahepatic bile ducts. The vessel is under sympathetic (vasoconstrictor) control through the hepatic plexus; blood flow is buffered by the hepatic arterial buffer response when portal flow falls.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: it is an artery; the wall is nourished by vasa vasorum. Venous drainage of the segment: the hepatic veins. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Hepatocellular carcinoma is hypervascular in the arterial phase, so transarterial chemoembolisation is delivered through segmental branches of this kind. Hepatic artery thrombosis (the most feared early complication of liver transplantation) causes bile duct ischaemia and strictures. Aneurysm or injury can follow percutaneous procedures and cholecystectomy. Hilar cholangiocarcinoma and gallbladder cancer commonly invade segment IVb.

### caudate-lobe-branch-of-right-hepatic-artery

### right-hepatic-duct
- latinName: Ductus hepaticus dexter
- summary: The short duct (about 1 cm) formed by the right anterior (segments V and VIII) and right posterior (segments VI and VII) sectoral ducts.
- location: At the porta hepatis, behind the right hepatic artery and portal vein branches; the posterior duct arches over the right portal vein to join the anterior duct. Variants are common: an aberrant right posterior duct joins the common hepatic or cystic duct in about 20 percent.
- function: Collects bile from the right liver and carries it toward the common hepatic duct.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the peribiliary plexus from the right hepatic and cystic arteries (end-arterial supply). Venous: peribiliary veins to the portal system. Lymph: hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Stricture or injury causes jaundice, and the ischaemia-prone supply makes bile duct injuries hard to repair. Aberrant right ducts are at risk in cholecystectomy. Hilar cholangiocarcinoma (Klatskin tumour) is classified by involvement of the confluence (Bismuth-Corlette).

### left-hepatic-duct
- latinName: Ductus hepaticus sinister
- summary: The duct (2 to 5 cm) formed by the ducts of segments II, III and IV, longer and more horizontal than the right.
- location: Runs along the base of segment IV, at the hilar plate, above the left portal vein, to join the right duct at the hilar confluence.
- function: Collects bile from the left liver and carries it toward the common hepatic duct.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the peribiliary plexus from the left hepatic artery. Venous: peribiliary veins to the portal system. Lymph: hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Its length makes it accessible for hepaticojejunostomy and a common first duct to dilate in obstruction. The hilar confluence is the site of Klatskin tumours.

### common-hepatic-duct
- latinName: Ductus hepaticus communis
- summary: The duct formed by the union of the right and left hepatic ducts, about 3 to 4 cm long, which becomes the common bile duct (about 7 to 8 cm, 6 mm wide) after the cystic duct joins it.
- location: In the free edge of the lesser omentum, to the right of the hepatic artery and in front of the portal vein; it forms the left boundary of Calot triangle.
- function: Carries bile out of the liver to the confluence with the cystic duct.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the cystic artery, right hepatic artery and posterior superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries (a vulnerable axial supply). Venous: portal system. Lymph: hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Mirizzi syndrome (a stone in the cystic duct or Hartmann pouch compressing it) causes obstructive jaundice. Bile duct injury in cholecystectomy (misidentifying it as the cystic duct) is classified by Strasberg. Cholangitis (Charcot triad: fever, jaundice, right upper quadrant pain) follows obstruction.

### cystic-duct
- latinName: Ductus cysticus
- summary: A short, tortuous duct (2 to 4 cm) with a spiral mucosal fold (valves of Heister) that connects the neck of the gallbladder to the common hepatic duct.
- location: In the hepatoduodenal ligament, forming the right side of the hepatocystic (Calot) triangle with the common hepatic duct and the liver edge; it joins the common hepatic duct at a variable level.
- function: Lets bile flow into and out of the gallbladder; the spiral folds keep it open. Autonomic control: vagal (contraction of the gallbladder) and sympathetic fibres.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the cystic artery. Venous: cystic vein to the portal vein. Lymph: cystic node (of Lund), then hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: A stone lodged here causes biliary colic and acute cholecystitis. It is clipped and divided in cholecystectomy after the critical view of safety is achieved. A long parallel course or low insertion increases surgical risk.

### gallbladder
- latinName: Vesica biliaris (fellea)
- summary: A pear-shaped sac, 7 to 10 cm long and holding 30 to 50 ml, with fundus, body, infundibulum (Hartmann pouch) and neck. The mucosa folds with Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses and there is no submucosa; peritoneum covers all but the liver-attached surface. It develops from the cystic bud of the hepatic diverticulum.
- location: On the visceral surface of the liver in the gallbladder fossa between segments IV and V on the Cantlie line; the fundus meets the ninth costal cartilage at the lateral border of rectus abdominis (the transpyloric plane). Relations: first and second parts of the duodenum, hepatic flexure and transverse colon.
- function: Concentrates bile five- to ten-fold by absorbing water and sodium, stores it, and contracts on cholecystokinin released by fatty food (with sphincter of Oddi relaxation). Innervation: vagus (contraction), sympathetic fibres from the coeliac plexus (T7 to T10); right phrenic referral causes right shoulder pain.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the cystic artery (right hepatic artery) from Calot triangle. Venous: cystic vein to the portal vein, with small veins directly to the liver. Lymph: cystic node (of Lund), then hepatic and coeliac nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Gallstones (cholesterol about 80 percent, or pigment), biliary colic, acute cholecystitis (Murphy sign, ultrasound wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid), choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, gallstone ileus, porcelain gallbladder and gallbladder cancer occur. Courvoisier law: a palpable, non-tender gallbladder with jaundice suggests malignant obstruction (usually pancreatic head cancer). Cholecystectomy is usually laparoscopic with the critical view of safety.

### anterior-superior-tributary-of-right-hepatic-biliary-tree
- latinName: Ductulus biliaris segmenti VIII
- summary: The segmental bile duct that drains segment VIII (anterior superior) into the right biliary system, lined by cholangiocytes (columnar epithelium). Bile canaliculi drain through the canals of Hering to interlobular and then segmental ducts. The ducts arise from the hepatic diverticulum of the foregut.
- location: In the portal pedicle of segment VIII, within the right anterior section, usually in front of and above the portal vein branch, in the upper anterior part of the right liver directly beneath the diaphragm.
- function: Carries bile (about 600 to 1000 ml a day) from segment VIII to the sectoral and then right hepatic duct, modifying it with bicarbonate-rich secretion under secretin control.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the peribiliary plexus fed by small hepatic artery branches only, so the ducts are vulnerable to ischaemia. Venous: peribiliary veins to the portal system. Lymph: periportal lymphatics to hepatic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Intrahepatic stones (hepatolithiasis), recurrent pyogenic cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis (beaded strictures), Caroli disease and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma involve segmental ducts. A cut duct causes bile leak after resection. Percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography accesses peripheral ducts. Its subdiaphragmatic position makes ablation and resection difficult and exposes it to hydatid cysts and amoebic abscesses that can rupture into the chest.

### pancreas
- latinName: Pancreas
- summary: A retroperitoneal gland 12 to 15 cm long (70 to 100 g) with a head (with the uncinate process), neck, body and tail. The exocrine acini make up about 85 percent and the islets of Langerhans (beta, alpha, delta and PP cells) 1 to 2 percent. It develops from a dorsal bud and a ventral bud of the foregut that fuse in the sixth to seventh week; the ventral bud forms the uncinate process and part of the head.
- location: At L1 to L2 (the transpyloric plane): the head sits in the duodenal C-loop, the neck lies in front of the portal and superior mesenteric vein confluence, the body crosses the aorta, the superior mesenteric artery origin, the left kidney and adrenal, and the tail reaches the splenic hilum in the splenorenal ligament. The stomach lies in front (lesser sac) and the splenic vein runs along its back; the common bile duct passes behind or through the head.
- function: Exocrine: digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, proteases) and bicarbonate, driven by cholecystokinin and secretin. Endocrine: insulin, glucagon, somatostatin and pancreatic polypeptide for glucose control. Parasympathetic fibres (vagus) stimulate secretion; sympathetic fibres from the splanchnic nerves carry pain (epigastric, radiating to the back).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: splenic artery branches (dorsal pancreatic, pancreatica magna, caudal), the superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries (gastroduodenal artery) and the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (superior mesenteric artery). Venous: splenic vein, superior mesenteric vein and portal vein. Lymph: pancreaticosplenic and pancreaticoduodenal nodes, then coeliac and superior mesenteric nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Acute pancreatitis (gallstones, alcohol; lipase more than three times normal; Cullen and Grey Turner signs; complications include necrosis, pseudocyst and organ failure), chronic pancreatitis (calcification, steatorrhoea, diabetes) and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (head: painless jaundice and Courvoisier sign; body and tail: late with back pain; CA 19-9; Whipple procedure) are major diseases. Neuroendocrine tumours include insulinoma and gastrinoma. Pancreas divisum and annular pancreas are congenital variants.

### parenchyma-of-pancreas
- latinName: Parenchyma pancreatis
- summary: The glandular tissue of the pancreas: exocrine acini of pyramidal cells with zymogen granules and centroacinar cells draining to intercalated ducts, and about one million islets of Langerhans scattered among them (more in the tail).
- location: Fills the lobules of the head, neck, body and tail around the duct system and is surrounded by a thin, lobulating connective tissue capsule.
- function: Acinar cells secrete inactive enzyme precursors (trypsinogen and others) activated in the duodenum; islet cells control glucose through insulin (beta), glucagon (alpha), somatostatin (delta) and pancreatic polypeptide (PP).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the splenic and pancreaticoduodenal arteries through a rich capillary network; islets receive a disproportionate 10 to 15 percent of the flow. Venous: portal system. Lymph: pancreatic lymphatics to splenic and coeliac nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Premature trypsinogen activation causes autodigestion in acute pancreatitis (hereditary forms: PRSS1, SPINK1). Cystic fibrosis causes thick secretions and exocrine insufficiency (low faecal elastase). Type 1 diabetes follows beta cell destruction, and type 3c diabetes follows pancreatic disease. Insulinoma (Whipple triad), glucagonoma (necrolytic migratory erythema) and fat replacement occur.

### pancreatic-duct
- latinName: Ductus pancreaticus (Wirsung)
- summary: The main pancreatic duct, 2 to 3 mm wide in the head, running the length of the gland and formed by the fusion of the ventral duct with the distal part of the dorsal duct; the proximal dorsal duct persists as the accessory duct (Santorini).
- location: Runs from the tail to the head through the middle of the gland, joins the common bile duct to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla (of Vater) and opens at the major duodenal papilla in the second part of the duodenum, about 8 to 10 cm from the pylorus, controlled by the sphincter of Oddi. The accessory duct opens at the minor papilla about 2 cm above it.
- function: Carries 1 to 1.5 litres of exocrine juice a day into the duodenum; the sphincter of Oddi is relaxed by cholecystokinin and controlled by vagal and sympathetic fibres.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the pancreaticoduodenal arcades. Venous: portal system. Lymph: pancreaticoduodenal nodes.
- clinicalNotes: A gallstone lodged at the ampulla causes gallstone pancreatitis; ERCP cannulation risks post-ERCP pancreatitis. A dilated common bile duct and pancreatic duct (double-duct sign) suggests a pancreatic head or ampullary tumour. Chronic pancreatitis shows a chain-of-lakes appearance; an intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm dilates the duct; pancreas divisum (dorsal duct draining most juice through the minor papilla) can cause pancreatitis.

### pancreatic-duct-tree
- latinName: Arbor ductuum pancreaticorum
- summary: The branching duct system of the pancreas: intercalated ducts from the acini, intralobular and interlobular ducts, the main pancreatic duct and the accessory duct, lined by cuboidal to columnar epithelium; centroacinar and ductal cells secrete bicarbonate-rich fluid through CFTR.
- location: Throughout the head, neck, body and tail, converging on the main duct, which joins the bile duct at the ampulla.
- function: Collects and neutralises pancreatic juice and conducts it to the duodenum, protecting the gland from premature enzyme activation.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: pancreatic arterial branches. Venous: portal system. Lymph: pancreatic and pancreaticoduodenal nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Cystic fibrosis blocks the small ducts with viscous secretions, leading to pancreatic insufficiency. Ductal adenocarcinoma arises from ductal epithelium (PanIN precursors). Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms, strictures and disruption of ducts in necrotising pancreatitis are imaged by MRCP and ERCP; endoscopic ultrasound samples suspicious lesions.
