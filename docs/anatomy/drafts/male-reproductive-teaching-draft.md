### right-testis
- latinName: Testis dexter
- summary: An ovoid gland about 4 to 5 cm long (15 to 25 ml) enclosed by the tunica albuginea, divided into 200 to 300 lobules of seminiferous tubules (Sertoli and spermatogenic cells) with interstitial Leydig cells; the tubules drain into the rete testis in the mediastinum testis. It develops from the gonadal ridge (intermediate mesoderm) and descends through the inguinal canal in the third trimester, guided by the gubernaculum, taking a pouch of peritoneum (the tunica vaginalis).
- location: In the scrotum, suspended by the spermatic cord, with the epididymis on its posterolateral surface. Scrotal wall layers from outside in: skin, dartos, external spermatic fascia (external oblique), cremasteric fascia (internal oblique), internal spermatic fascia (transversalis fascia) and the tunica vaginalis.
- function: Spermatogenesis in the seminiferous tubules (about 74 days) requires a temperature 2 to 3 degrees below core; Sertoli cells form the blood-testis barrier and secrete inhibin B (FSH-driven); Leydig cells secrete testosterone (LH-driven). Autonomic supply: testicular plexus (sympathetic T10 to L1). Testicular pain refers to the T10 dermatome; the anterior scrotum is supplied by the ilioinguinal nerve and genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve, the posterior scrotum by the pudendal nerve; the cremasteric reflex (L1 to L2) travels in the genitofemoral nerve.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: testicular artery from the abdominal aorta (about L2), with cremasteric and ductus deferens artery contributions. Venous: pampiniform plexus draining to the right testicular vein, which enters the inferior vena cava obliquely below the renal vein. Lymph: lateral aortic (para-aortic) nodes at L1 to L2, not the inguinal nodes (scrotal skin drains to superficial inguinal nodes).
- clinicalNotes: Testicular torsion (bell-clapper deformity; sudden severe pain, high-riding transverse lie, absent cremasteric reflex) is a surgical emergency with a salvage window of about 6 hours. Epididymo-orchitis, hydrocele (transilluminates) and cryptorchidism (raises cancer risk; orchidopexy) occur. A right-sided varicocele is uncommon and should prompt a search for a retroperitoneal mass or inferior vena cava obstruction. Testicular germ cell tumours (seminoma, non-seminoma; markers AFP, beta-hCG, LDH) spread to para-aortic nodes, and orchidectomy is by an inguinal approach.

### right-epididymis
- latinName: Epididymis dexter
- summary: A single, tightly coiled tube (about 6 m uncoiled) with head (efferent ductules), body and tail, lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium with stereocilia. It develops from the cranial part of the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct and the efferent ductules from mesonephric tubules.
- location: On the posterolateral surface of the right testis within the scrotum, with the head at the upper pole and the tail continuing as the ductus deferens. The sinus of the epididymis lies between it and the testis.
- function: Matures sperm (motility and fertilising ability, over about 10 to 14 days), stores them in the tail and reabsorbs testicular fluid. Smooth muscle contraction during emission is under sympathetic control (T10 to L1). Sensation travels with the genitofemoral and testicular plexus fibres.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: testicular artery and the artery of the ductus deferens. Venous: pampiniform plexus. Lymph: external iliac and para-aortic nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Epididymitis causes painful swelling that is relieved by elevation (Prehn sign): sexually transmitted organisms (Chlamydia, Neisseria) in men under 35 and enteric bacteria (E. coli) in older men; tuberculosis can cause a chronic form. Spermatoceles and epididymal cysts are common. Vasectomy obstructs the ductus deferens and congests the epididymis.

### right-seminal-vesicle
- latinName: Vesicula seminalis dexter
- summary: A coiled glandular pouch about 5 cm long (about 10 cm uncoiled) with a pseudostratified columnar secretory epithelium. It develops as an outgrowth of the mesonephric duct.
- location: Extraperitoneal on the posterior surface of the bladder base on the right side, lateral to the ampulla of the ductus deferens, above the prostate and in front of the rectum, separated from it by the rectovesical (Denonvilliers) fascia. Its duct joins the ductus deferens to form the ejaculatory duct, which passes through the prostate to open at the colliculus seminalis.
- function: Secretes an alkaline, fructose-rich fluid (about 50 to 70 percent of semen volume) that contains prostaglandins and seminogelin, which supports sperm motility and semen coagulation. Emission is driven by sympathetic fibres (T10 to L2, hypogastric nerves) and secretion by parasympathetic fibres (S2 to S4).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: inferior vesical and middle rectal arteries (internal iliac) and the artery of the ductus deferens. Venous: vesical and prostatic venous plexus to the internal iliac vein. Lymph: internal and external iliac nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Vesiculitis and haematospermia occur. Invasion by prostate cancer (T3b) is a staging feature and is best seen on MRI. Congenital absence of the vas deferens and seminal vesicle occurs in cystic fibrosis (CFTR mutations), and seminal vesicle cysts can accompany ipsilateral renal agenesis (Zinner syndrome). The vesicles are at risk in radical prostatectomy and are palpable on rectal examination only when enlarged.

### prostate
- latinName: Prostata
- summary: A walnut-sized gland (about 20 g) of 30 to 50 tubuloalveolar glands in a fibromuscular stroma, with a capsule. McNeal zones: peripheral (about 70 percent of the gland, where most cancers arise), central, transition (benign hyperplasia) and anterior fibromuscular stroma. It develops from endodermal buds of the urogenital sinus under dihydrotestosterone (5-alpha-reductase).
- location: Below the bladder neck, surrounding the prostatic urethra, with the base up against the bladder and the apex resting on the external urethral sphincter. Anteriorly: the pubic symphysis and retropubic space (of Retzius). Posteriorly: the rectum, separated by Denonvilliers fascia, so the posterior surface is palpable on digital rectal examination. Laterally: levator ani.
- function: Secretes about 25 to 30 percent of semen: prostate-specific antigen (PSA, liquefies semen), citrate, zinc and acid phosphatase. Smooth muscle contracts at ejaculation and closes the bladder neck. Innervation: inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus with sympathetic (T10 to L2) and parasympathetic (S2 to S4) fibres; the cavernous nerves for erection run posterolaterally in the neurovascular bundles.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: inferior vesical, middle rectal and internal pudendal arteries. Venous: the prostatic venous plexus (Santorini), draining to the internal iliac veins and communicating with the vertebral venous plexus of Batson. Lymph: internal iliac, obturator and sacral nodes, and external iliac nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (transition zone) causes frequency, nocturia, weak stream and retention; treated with alpha-blockers, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors or transurethral resection (TURP; TUR syndrome from fluid absorption). Prostate adenocarcinoma arises mostly in the peripheral zone (hard, nodular, asymmetric on rectal examination; PSA; Gleason grade) and spreads to bone (osteoblastic) through Batson plexus. Nerve-sparing prostatectomy aims to preserve erection and continence. Prostatitis causes a tender, boggy gland.

### glans-penis
- latinName: Glans penis
- summary: The distal expansion of the corpus spongiosum, with the corona, neck, frenulum and the external urethral meatus at the tip (the navicular fossa lies just inside). It is covered by thin skin continuous with the inner layer of the prepuce and is rich in sensory corpuscles. It develops from the genital tubercle.
- location: At the distal end of the penis, covered by the prepuce (foreskin) in uncircumcised males. The ends of the corpora cavernosa fit into a concavity on its deep surface.
- function: Provides sensation for sexual function and directs the urinary stream and semen. Sensory supply: the dorsal nerve of the penis (pudendal nerve, S2 to S4). It becomes engorged in erection through the cavernous and dorsal arteries.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: dorsal arteries of the penis and the bulbourethral and urethral arteries (internal pudendal). Venous: deep dorsal vein of the penis to the prostatic venous plexus. Lymph: deep inguinal nodes (shaft skin drains to superficial inguinal nodes).
- clinicalNotes: Balanitis and balanoposthitis (candida, diabetes), phimosis, paraphimosis (a retracted foreskin that cannot be replaced; an emergency), hypospadias (ventral meatus from incomplete urethral fold fusion) and epispadias occur. Penile squamous cell carcinoma is linked to HPV, poor hygiene and lichen sclerosus. Circumcision lowers the risk of balanitis and some infections.

### corpus-cavernosum-of-penis
- latinName: Corpus cavernosum penis
- summary: One of a pair of cylinders of erectile tissue: smooth muscle trabeculae and connective tissue enclosing endothelium-lined cavernous sinusoids, surrounded by the thick, bilayered tunica albuginea and Buck fascia. The pair are separated by an incomplete septum, and their proximal ends (crura) attach to the ischiopubic rami.
- location: On the dorsal and lateral parts of the penile shaft, side by side, with the corpus spongiosum in the groove between them below. The crura are covered by the ischiocavernosus muscles in the perineum.
- function: Erection: parasympathetic fibres (S2 to S4, pelvic splanchnic nerves, nervi erigentes) release nitric oxide, increasing cGMP and relaxing trabecular smooth muscle; the sinusoids fill and compress the emissary veins against the tunica albuginea (veno-occlusion). Detumescence follows sympathetic vasoconstriction (T11 to L2). The somatic pudendal nerve drives the ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles for rigidity.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: deep arteries of the penis (central helicine arteries) and the dorsal arteries, from the internal pudendal artery. Venous: deep dorsal vein, cavernous veins and crural veins to the internal pudendal and prostatic plexus. Lymph: superficial and deep inguinal nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Erectile dysfunction (vascular, diabetic, neurogenic or drug-related) is treated with PDE5 inhibitors, which are contraindicated with nitrates. Penile fracture (rupture of the tunica albuginea, with a snap, rapid detumescence and an eggplant-shaped haematoma) needs urgent surgery. Priapism (ischaemic type: painful, rigid; seen in sickle cell disease) is an emergency. Peyronie disease is a fibrous plaque of the tunica albuginea.

### corpus-spongiosum-of-penis
- latinName: Corpus spongiosum penis
- summary: A single column of erectile tissue surrounding the spongy (penile) urethra, with a thinner tunica albuginea and less rigid engorgement. It expands proximally as the bulb of the penis and distally as the glans.
- location: On the ventral side of the penis in the groove between the corpora cavernosa. The bulb lies in the superficial perineal pouch, attached to the perineal membrane and covered by the bulbospongiosus muscle; the urethra enters its upper surface.
- function: Keeps the urethra patent during erection so that semen can pass, and forms the glans. Rhythmic contraction of the bulbospongiosus (pudendal nerve, S2 to S4) expels semen and urine. Autonomic supply: the cavernous nerves.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: artery of the bulb and the urethral artery (internal pudendal), with the dorsal artery to the glans. Venous: bulbar and deep dorsal veins to the internal pudendal veins and prostatic plexus. Lymph: deep inguinal nodes for the glans, superficial inguinal nodes for the skin.
- clinicalNotes: A straddle injury crushes the bulb against the pubic bone and ruptures the bulbar urethra (blood at the meatus, urinary retention, butterfly-shaped perineal haematoma contained by Colles fascia), unlike the membranous urethra, which is injured in pelvic fractures. Urethral strictures follow infection, catheterisation or trauma. Urethritis and urethral cancer are less common.
