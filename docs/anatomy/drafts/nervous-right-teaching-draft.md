# MD-level teaching text: nervous-right (21 structures)

**Status: AI-drafted, pending faculty review.** Original wording, written from general anatomical knowledge, not copied from any textbook and not yet verified by faculty. Load as `isReviewed=false`.

Field use: summary (structure, histology, embryology); location (levels and relations); function (physiology and connections); bloodSupply (artery, vein, lymph); clinicalNotes (pathology, imaging, procedures). British spelling. Left and right are the patient's. The language-dominant hemisphere is the left in about 95 percent of right-handed and about 70 percent of left-handed people; entries describe the dominant (left) and non-dominant (right) side separately.

Common to all cortical structures: neocortex has six layers and arises from the telencephalon. Venous drainage is through superficial cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus (superior surface) or the superficial middle cerebral vein and the transverse and sigmoid sinuses (lateral and inferior surfaces), and through deep veins to the internal cerebral vein, great cerebral vein and straight sinus. The brain has no conventional lymphatic vessels; interstitial fluid drains through CSF, perivascular and meningeal lymphatic routes. Arterial territories: anterior cerebral artery (medial frontal and parietal surface), middle cerebral artery (lateral surface and insula), posterior cerebral artery (occipital lobe and inferior temporal surface).

### white-matter-of-right-cerebral-hemisphere
- latinName: Substantia alba hemispherii cerebri dexter
- summary: Myelinated axons (myelin from oligodendrocytes) that make up about half of the brain volume, organised as association fibres (short U-fibres and long bundles such as the arcuate, superior longitudinal, uncinate, inferior fronto-occipital and cingulum), commissural fibres (corpus callosum, anterior commissure) and projection fibres (corona radiata to internal capsule). Myelination continues into the third decade.
- location: Deep to the right cortex: the centrum semiovale above the lateral ventricle, fanning into the corona radiata above the basal ganglia and thalamus, and condensing into the internal capsule.
- function: Connects cortical regions within the hemisphere and across the midline. In the right hemisphere long association tracts support spatial attention and prosody.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: long medullary branches of the middle, anterior and posterior cerebral arteries, with deep penetrators (lenticulostriate, anterior choroidal). The deep watershed between anterior and middle cerebral territories lies in the centrum semiovale. Venous: medullary veins to subependymal veins and the internal cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Multiple sclerosis (periventricular plaques, Dawson fingers), small vessel ischaemic disease (periventricular hyperintensities, Fazekas scale), progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, leukodystrophies and diffuse axonal injury are white matter disorders. Hypotension causes watershed infarcts (man-in-a-barrel pattern). Diffusion tensor tractography maps tracts before tumour surgery.

### right-internal-capsule
- latinName: Capsula interna dexter
- summary: A compact V-shaped band of white matter with an anterior limb (between the head of the caudate and the lentiform nucleus), genu and posterior limb (between the thalamus and lentiform nucleus), plus sublenticular and retrolenticular parts.
- location: Between the right caudate and thalamus medially and the lentiform nucleus laterally, continuous above with the corona radiata and below with the cerebral peduncle.
- function: Carries frontopontine and anterior thalamic fibres (anterior limb), corticobulbar fibres (genu), corticospinal fibres (anterior two thirds of the posterior limb) and thalamocortical sensory fibres (posterior third of the posterior limb); the optic radiation passes through the retrolenticular part and auditory radiation through the sublenticular part.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the medial striate artery (recurrent artery of Heubner, from the anterior cerebral artery) and lenticulostriate branches (middle cerebral artery) for the anterior limb and genu; lenticulostriate and anterior choroidal arteries for the posterior limb. Venous: thalamostriate and internal cerebral veins.
- clinicalNotes: A lacunar infarct or haemorrhage in the posterior limb causes pure motor hemiparesis of the left face, arm and leg because fibres are densely packed; ataxic hemiparesis and pure sensory stroke are other lacunar syndromes. Anterior choroidal artery occlusion gives contralateral hemiparesis, hemisensory loss and homonymous hemianopia. Hypertensive putaminal haemorrhage often extends into it. The anterior limb is a deep brain stimulation target in refractory obsessive-compulsive disorder.

### right-superior-frontal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus frontalis superior dexter
- summary: The uppermost gyrus of the frontal lobe, mostly Brodmann areas 6, 8 and 9. Its medial and posterior part contains the supplementary motor area.
- location: Above the superior frontal sulcus on the superior and medial surface of the right frontal lobe, running up to the cingulate sulcus and in front of the paracentral lobule.
- function: Planning and initiation of movement (supplementary motor area), working memory, self-awareness and executive control.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: frontopolar and callosomarginal branches of the anterior cerebral artery (medial surface) and prefrontal branches of the middle cerebral artery (lateral part). Venous: superior cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Anterior cerebral artery stroke causes left leg weakness, abulia, urinary incontinence and sometimes an alien hand. Supplementary motor area lesions cause transient akinesia. Falcine meningiomas arise next to it.

### right-superior-parietal-lobule
- latinName: Lobulus parietalis superior dexter
- summary: The upper part of the parietal lobe above the intraparietal sulcus (Brodmann areas 5 and 7), continuous medially with the precuneus.
- location: Behind the postcentral gyrus and above the intraparietal sulcus on the right parietal lobe, adjacent to the occipital lobe behind and the paracentral lobule medially.
- function: Integrates somatosensory and visual input to represent space and the body; the right (non-dominant) side is central to spatial attention.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: posterior parietal branches of the middle cerebral artery and pericallosal branches of the anterior cerebral artery, with a border zone supplied by the posterior cerebral artery. Venous: superior cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Right-sided lesions cause left hemispatial neglect, anosognosia and constructional apraxia (impaired clock drawing and copying), as well as optic ataxia and astereognosis.

### right-occipital-lobe
- latinName: Lobus occipitalis dexter
- summary: The most posterior lobe, containing the primary visual cortex (V1, Brodmann area 17) on the banks of the calcarine sulcus and the visual association cortex (areas 18 and 19). The calcarine sulcus separates the cuneus above from the lingual gyrus below.
- location: At the posterior pole of the right hemisphere, behind the parieto-occipital sulcus, under the tentorium cerebelli and above the transverse sinus.
- function: Processes vision from the opposite visual field: the lower field is represented on the cuneus (above the calcarine sulcus) and the upper field on the lingual gyrus; central (macular) vision has a disproportionately large representation at the pole. Input comes from the lateral geniculate nucleus through the optic radiation.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: calcarine and parieto-occipital branches of the posterior cerebral artery, with collateral supply from the middle cerebral artery to the occipital pole. Venous: occipital veins to the transverse and straight sinuses.
- clinicalNotes: Posterior cerebral artery stroke causes a left homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing (collateral supply at the pole). A lesion of the cuneus gives an inferior quadrantanopia and of the lingual gyrus a superior one. Bilateral occipital lesions cause cortical blindness (Anton syndrome). Occipital epilepsy and migraine aura cause visual phenomena.

### right-precentral-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus precentralis dexter
- summary: The primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4), with large pyramidal Betz cells in layer V. It carries a body map (motor homunculus) with the face and hand over the lateral convexity (the hand knob) and the leg and foot on the medial surface.
- location: In front of the central sulcus on the right frontal lobe, behind the premotor cortex (area 6) and continuous medially with the paracentral lobule.
- function: Sends voluntary movement commands to the opposite side of the body through the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts. Planning comes from the premotor and supplementary motor areas and feedback from the basal ganglia and cerebellum through the thalamus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: precentral and central branches of the middle cerebral artery (face, arm and hand) and the paracentral branch of the anterior cerebral artery (leg and foot). Venous: superior cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: A middle cerebral artery stroke causes left face and arm weakness greater than leg; an anterior cerebral artery stroke affects the left leg most. Focal motor seizures spread along the homunculus (Jacksonian march), followed by temporary weakness (Todd paresis). Upper motor neuron signs follow: spasticity, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar response. Motor cortex mapping guides tumour resection.

### right-postcentral-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus postcentralis dexter
- summary: The primary somatosensory cortex (Brodmann areas 3, 1 and 2) with a sensory body map (sensory homunculus) in which the lips, tongue and hand have large representations.
- location: Behind the central sulcus on the right parietal lobe, in front of the superior parietal lobule and continuous medially with the paracentral lobule.
- function: Receives touch, vibration, pressure, position and, to a lesser degree, pain and temperature information from the opposite side of the body through the ventral posterolateral and ventral posteromedial nuclei of the thalamus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: postcentral and anterior parietal branches of the middle cerebral artery (lateral surface) and pericallosal branches of the anterior cerebral artery (leg area). Venous: superior cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Lesions cause cortical sensory loss on the left: astereognosis, agraphaesthesia, impaired two-point discrimination and position sense, with relatively spared pain and crude touch. Sensory focal seizures cause tingling that spreads along the homunculus.

### right-angular-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus angularis dexter
- summary: A gyrus of the inferior parietal lobule (Brodmann area 39) that caps the posterior end of the superior temporal sulcus.
- location: At the junction of the right parietal, temporal and occipital lobes, behind the supramarginal gyrus.
- function: Contributes to attention, spatial cognition and body schema, and to metaphor and social cognition.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the angular branch of the middle cerebral artery (inferior division), at the border zone with the posterior cerebral artery. Venous: superficial middle cerebral and superior anastomotic veins.
- clinicalNotes: A right-sided lesion contributes to hemispatial neglect, anosognosia and disorientation, and can cause out-of-body experiences when stimulated.

### right-middle-frontal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus frontalis medius dexter
- summary: The middle horizontal gyrus of the lateral frontal lobe (Brodmann areas 9, 46 and the posterior part of 8, which contains the frontal eye field).
- location: Between the superior and inferior frontal sulci on the right frontal lobe, in front of the precentral gyrus.
- function: The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex supports working memory, planning, attention and cognitive flexibility; the frontal eye field (area 8) initiates voluntary saccades toward the opposite side.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: prefrontal and precentral branches of the middle cerebral artery (superior division). Venous: superior cerebral veins to the superior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Dorsolateral lesions cause a dysexecutive syndrome with poor planning, perseveration and apathy. A destructive frontal eye field lesion deviates the eyes toward the side of the lesion; an irritative focus (seizure) deviates them away. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is the transcranial magnetic stimulation target in depression.

### right-inferior-frontal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus frontalis inferior dexter
- summary: The lowest gyrus on the lateral frontal lobe, with orbital (Brodmann area 47), triangular (45) and opercular (44) parts. In the dominant hemisphere areas 44 and 45 form Broca area.
- location: Below the inferior frontal sulcus on the right frontal lobe and above the lateral fissure, in front of the ventral precentral gyrus.
- function: Contributes to prosody, response inhibition and the interpretation of emotional tone of speech.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: prefrontal and precentral branches of the superior division of the middle cerebral artery. Venous: superficial middle cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: A lesion causes aprosodia (flat, monotone speech or inability to convey emotion), with preserved words; right-sided inferior frontal damage also impairs inhibition.

### right-inferior-temporal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus temporalis inferior dexter
- summary: The lowest gyrus on the lateral surface of the temporal lobe, part of the ventral visual (what) stream, around Brodmann areas 20 and 21.
- location: Along the inferolateral edge of the right temporal lobe, below the middle temporal gyrus and extending onto the inferior surface next to the fusiform gyrus.
- function: Recognises complex visual objects, faces and categories; neurons here respond to shape and identity regardless of position and size.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: temporal branches of the inferior division of the middle cerebral artery and the inferior temporal branches of the posterior cerebral artery. Venous: inferior anastomotic vein (of Labbe) to the transverse sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Damage causes visual object agnosia and contributes to prosopagnosia. The vein of Labbe drains it, and thrombosis of the transverse sinus can cause venous infarction here. A common site of temporal lobe epilepsy foci and temporal lobectomy approaches.

### right-middle-temporal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus temporalis medius dexter
- summary: The middle gyrus of the lateral temporal lobe (Brodmann area 21), between the superior and inferior temporal sulci. Area V5 (MT), a motion processing region, lies at its posterior end near the occipital junction.
- location: On the lateral surface of the right temporal lobe, below the superior temporal gyrus and above the inferior temporal gyrus.
- function: Recognition of faces and nonverbal sounds, social perception, and motion perception through area V5.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: temporal branches of the inferior division of the middle cerebral artery. Venous: superficial middle cerebral vein and the vein of Labbe.
- clinicalNotes: Right-sided damage impairs recognising faces and nonverbal sounds; it is among the early regions of atrophy in Alzheimer disease. A lesion of area V5 causes akinetopsia (loss of motion perception).

### right-insula
- latinName: Insula dexter
- summary: A cortical island buried in the lateral sulcus, divided by the central insular sulcus into short and long gyri and covered by the frontal, parietal and temporal opercula; the claustrum and extreme capsule lie deep to it.
- location: Deep in the lateral fissure of the right hemisphere, over the lentiform nucleus, with the circular sulcus of the insula around its margin.
- function: The anterior insula and frontal operculum contain primary gustatory cortex and mediate interoception, emotional awareness and disgust; the posterior insula processes pain, temperature and visceral sensation. Autonomic control of heart rate and blood pressure is lateralised, with the right insula more sympathetic.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: insular (M2) branches of the middle cerebral artery. Venous: deep middle cerebral vein draining to the basal vein.
- clinicalNotes: Insular infarcts cause dysgeusia, arrhythmia and blood pressure changes (right insular lesions are linked with a higher risk of cardiac arrhythmia), and dysphagia; loss of the insular ribbon (grey-white differentiation) is an early CT sign of middle cerebral artery infarction. Central post-stroke pain can follow a lesion here.

### right-cingulate-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus cinguli dexter
- summary: A curved gyrus of the limbic lobe on the medial surface, wrapping above the corpus callosum (Brodmann areas 23, 24, 25, 29 to 33), with the cingulum bundle running within it. The anterior part concerns attention and emotion; the posterior part belongs to the default mode network.
- location: On the medial wall of the right hemisphere, between the callosal sulcus below and the cingulate sulcus above, from the subcallosal area to the isthmus and parahippocampal gyrus.
- function: The anterior cingulate monitors conflict, attention and the affective component of pain and motivates behaviour; the posterior cingulate and precuneus support memory retrieval and self-referential thought. It forms part of the Papez circuit.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: pericallosal and callosomarginal branches of the anterior cerebral artery. Venous: internal cerebral and superior sagittal sinus tributaries, and the inferior sagittal sinus.
- clinicalNotes: Bilateral anterior cingulate lesions (anterior cerebral artery infarction) cause akinetic mutism. Subfalcine herniation compresses the cingulate gyrus and the anterior cerebral artery, causing leg weakness. Posterior cingulate hypometabolism on PET is an early sign of Alzheimer disease. Cingulotomy was used for refractory pain and obsessive-compulsive disorder, and subcallosal cingulate stimulation for depression.

### right-supramarginal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus supramarginalis dexter
- summary: A gyrus of the inferior parietal lobule (Brodmann area 40) that arches over the posterior end of the lateral sulcus.
- location: Behind the postcentral gyrus on the right parietal lobe, above the posterior superior temporal gyrus and in front of the angular gyrus.
- function: Contributes to spatial attention, body representation and empathy (recognising others' emotional states).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: anterior and posterior parietal branches of the inferior division of the middle cerebral artery. Venous: superficial middle cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: A right-sided lesion contributes to contralateral hemispatial neglect and difficulties in body schema and empathy.

### right-fusiform-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus fusiformis dexter
- summary: A long gyrus (occipitotemporal gyrus, Brodmann area 37) on the underside of the temporal and occipital lobes, between the collateral sulcus and the occipitotemporal sulcus.
- location: On the inferior surface of the right hemisphere, lateral to the parahippocampal gyrus and medial to the inferior temporal gyrus.
- function: The fusiform face area, most developed on the right, recognises faces; the region near V4 processes colour and the right side supports object recognition.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: inferior temporal and posterior temporal branches of the posterior cerebral artery. Venous: inferior cerebral veins to the transverse and petrosal sinuses.
- clinicalNotes: A right (or bilateral) lesion causes prosopagnosia, inability to recognise familiar faces despite intact vision; bilateral lesions also cause cerebral achromatopsia.

### right-hippocampus
- latinName: Hippocampus dexter
- summary: A curved, three-layered archicortical structure of the medial temporal lobe with the dentate gyrus and the subfields CA1 to CA4 and subiculum. The trisynaptic circuit passes from the entorhinal cortex (perforant path) to the dentate gyrus (mossy fibres) to CA3 (Schaffer collaterals) to CA1, then to the subiculum and back to the cortex; output travels through the fimbria and fornix to the mammillary bodies in the Papez circuit.
- location: In the floor of the temporal horn of the right lateral ventricle, with the choroid fissure above, the parahippocampal gyrus below and the amygdala anteriorly (the pes hippocampi at the front).
- function: Consolidates new declarative memories, especially visuospatial memory on the right, and supports spatial navigation through place cells. The dentate gyrus is one of the few sites of adult neurogenesis.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: hippocampal branches of the posterior cerebral artery and the anterior choroidal artery. Venous: hippocampal veins to the basal vein of Rosenthal.
- clinicalNotes: Hippocampal atrophy (the medial temporal atrophy score on MRI) is an early sign of Alzheimer disease. Mesial temporal sclerosis causes temporal lobe epilepsy treated by amygdalohippocampectomy. The CA1 (Sommer) sector is selectively vulnerable to hypoxia and ischaemia. Bilateral damage causes dense anterograde amnesia (patient H.M.). Herpes simplex encephalitis and limbic encephalitis (anti-NMDA receptor) target it.

### right-parahippocampal-gyrus
- latinName: Gyrus parahippocampalis dexter
- summary: A gyrus on the medial inferior temporal lobe containing the entorhinal cortex (Brodmann area 28), perirhinal and parahippocampal cortex, ending anteriorly in the hook-shaped uncus. It is the gateway between the neocortex and the hippocampal formation.
- location: On the medial surface of the right temporal lobe, between the hippocampal fissure above and the collateral sulcus below, with its uncus lying against the free edge of the tentorium and the midbrain.
- function: Relays information between association cortex and the hippocampus for memory encoding and retrieval; the parahippocampal place area processes scenes and spatial layout. The uncus contains olfactory cortex.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: anterior and posterior temporal branches of the posterior cerebral artery, with the anterior choroidal artery supplying the uncus. Venous: inferior cerebral veins and the basal vein.
- clinicalNotes: Transtentorial (uncal) herniation pushes the uncus and parahippocampal gyrus over the tentorial edge, compressing the ipsilateral oculomotor nerve (a dilated pupil), the posterior cerebral artery (occipital infarct) and the opposite cerebral peduncle (Kernohan notch, causing false-localising ipsilateral hemiparesis). The entorhinal cortex shows the first tau pathology of Alzheimer disease (Braak stages I and II). Uncinate fits cause olfactory hallucinations and deja vu.

### right-superior-colliculus
- latinName: Colliculus superior dexter
- summary: The rostral of the paired tectal elevations of the midbrain, with seven alternating cellular and fibrous layers that map the opposite visual field. It receives retinal, visual cortical and auditory inputs.
- location: On the dorsal midbrain on the right side of the midline, above the inferior colliculus, at the level of the oculomotor nuclei and below the pineal body; the pretectal area lies rostral to it.
- function: Directs rapid saccades and head and body orienting movements toward visual and auditory stimuli, through the tectospinal and tectobulbar tracts and the paramedian pontine reticular formation and oculomotor nuclei. The adjacent pretectum mediates the pupillary light reflex.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the quadrigeminal (collicular) branches of the posterior cerebral artery (P1 and P2) and branches of the superior cerebellar artery. Venous: the great cerebral vein and basal vein.
- clinicalNotes: Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome from pineal tumours, hydrocephalus or midbrain stroke causes impaired upgaze, convergence-retraction nystagmus, eyelid retraction (Collier sign) and light-near dissociation; in infants with hydrocephalus the setting-sun sign appears. Collicular lesions impair saccade initiation. Blindsight is thought to involve this pathway.

### right-inferior-colliculus
- latinName: Colliculus inferior dexter
- summary: The caudal paired tectal elevation of the midbrain, a major auditory relay with a central nucleus, a dorsal cortex and an external cortex.
- location: On the dorsal midbrain on the right side of the midline, below the superior colliculus; the trochlear nerve emerges just below it.
- function: Integrates ascending auditory input from the lateral lemniscus and relays it through the brachium of the inferior colliculus to the medial geniculate body and auditory cortex; it localises sounds from interaural timing and level differences and mediates the auditory startle reflex.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: collicular branches of the quadrigeminal artery (posterior cerebral) and the superior cerebellar artery. Venous: the great cerebral vein and basal vein.
- clinicalNotes: Because auditory pathways are bilateral, unilateral lesions cause little deafness but impair sound localisation; bilateral lesions cause central deafness and auditory agnosia. Pineal tumours and the top of the basilar syndrome can damage it. It lies next to the exit of the trochlear nerve.

### right-lateral-ventricle
- latinName: Ventriculus lateralis dexter
- summary: The CSF-filled cavity of the telencephalon, C-shaped with a frontal (anterior) horn, body, atrium (trigone), occipital (posterior) horn and temporal (inferior) horn, lined by ependyma. Choroid plexus lies in the body, atrium and temporal horn but not in the frontal and occipital horns.
- location: Deep in the right cerebral hemisphere: the roof is the corpus callosum, the frontal horn is bounded by the head of the caudate nucleus laterally and the septum pellucidum medially, the body lies over the thalamus, and the temporal horn lies in the temporal lobe with the hippocampus in its floor. It communicates with the third ventricle through the interventricular foramen (of Monro).
- function: The choroid plexus produces most of the CSF (about 500 ml a day in total), which cushions the brain, clears waste and maintains the chemical environment; CSF flows through the foramina into the third ventricle.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: anterior choroidal artery (internal carotid) and lateral posterior choroidal artery (posterior cerebral) to the choroid plexus. Venous: choroidal and thalamostriate veins draining to the internal cerebral vein.
- clinicalNotes: Obstructive hydrocephalus (aqueductal stenosis, colloid cyst, posterior fossa tumour) and communicating hydrocephalus (impaired absorption at arachnoid granulations; normal pressure hydrocephalus with gait disturbance, dementia and incontinence) enlarge the ventricles; enlarged temporal horns are an early sign. Intraventricular haemorrhage affects premature infants from the germinal matrix. A ventriculoperitoneal shunt is placed through the frontal horn (Kocher point) or occipital horn. Choroid plexus papilloma and ventriculitis occur. The septum pellucidum may contain a cavum.
