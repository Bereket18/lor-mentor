# MD-level teaching text: sensory-eye-right (21 structures)

**Status: AI-drafted, pending faculty review.** Original wording, written from general anatomical knowledge, not copied from any textbook and not yet verified by faculty. Load as `isReviewed=false`.

Field use: summary (structure, histology, embryology); location (levels and relations); function (physiology and innervation); bloodSupply (artery, vein, lymph); clinicalNotes (pathology, imaging, procedures). British spelling. Left and right are the patient's. The 3D model has no separate anterior chamber for the right eye (it is described in the left-eye file).

Common to the eye: the ophthalmic artery (internal carotid) supplies the orbit and globe through the central retinal, posterior ciliary, lacrimal, muscular and other branches; venous return passes through the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins to the cavernous sinus (and facially through the angular vein). The globe has no lymphatic vessels; conjunctival and eyelid lymph drains to the preauricular (parotid) nodes laterally and submandibular nodes medially. Sensory supply is from the ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) divisions of the trigeminal nerve.

### right-sclera
- latinName: Sclera
- summary: The opaque white outer coat of the globe, made of irregular type I collagen bundles in layers (episclera, stroma, lamina fusca), 0.3 to 1 mm thick (thinnest behind the rectus muscle insertions). The lamina cribrosa is the sieve-like part pierced by optic nerve fibres. It derives from neural crest and mesoderm.
- location: The posterior five sixths of the right globe, continuous with the cornea at the limbus and with the dural sheath of the optic nerve; the extraocular muscles insert into it and Tenon capsule surrounds it.
- function: Gives the eye its shape, resists intraocular pressure (normally 10 to 21 mmHg), protects the contents and anchors the muscles. Sensory supply: long and short ciliary nerves (V1).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: anterior ciliary arteries (from the muscular branches) forming the episcleral plexus, and posterior ciliary arteries; the sclera itself is relatively avascular. Venous: vortex veins and anterior ciliary veins. Lymph: no lymphatics in the globe.
- clinicalNotes: Scleritis causes severe boring pain and violaceous redness (rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis); episcleritis is benign and blanches with phenylephrine. Blue sclera occurs in osteogenesis imperfecta; scleral thinning and staphyloma in high myopia; rupture after blunt trauma. A scleral buckle is used in retinal detachment surgery.

### right-choroid
- latinName: Choroidea
- summary: A thin, highly vascular, pigmented layer of the uvea (with the iris and ciliary body), with Haller (large vessel), Sattler (medium vessel) and choriocapillaris layers, and Bruch membrane against the retinal pigment epithelium.
- location: Between the sclera (suprachoroidal space) and the retina, from the optic disc to the ora serrata, where it becomes the ciliary body of the right eye.
- function: Supplies oxygen and nutrients to the outer retina (photoreceptors), has the highest blood flow per unit tissue in the body, helps regulate retinal temperature and absorbs stray light. Innervation: parasympathetic (pterygopalatine ganglion), sympathetic and trigeminal sensory fibres.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: short posterior ciliary arteries (ophthalmic artery). Venous: four vortex veins to the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins and cavernous sinus. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Choroidal melanoma is the commonest primary intraocular malignancy in adults. Choroiditis (posterior uveitis), central serous chorioretinopathy, choroidal neovascularisation (wet age-related macular degeneration), choroidal rupture after trauma and choroideraemia occur. Trauma can cause choroidal haemorrhage or detachment.

### suspensory-ligament-of-right-lens
- latinName: Zonula ciliaris
- summary: A ring of acellular zonular fibres (fibrillin-1 microfibrils) running from the pars plana and the ciliary processes to the equator of the lens capsule.
- location: In the posterior chamber of the right eye, between the ciliary body and the lens equator, bathed by aqueous humour.
- function: Transmits ciliary muscle action to the lens. Contraction of the ciliary muscle (parasympathetic, oculomotor nerve via the ciliary ganglion) slackens the zonule, so the elastic lens rounds up for near vision; relaxation tightens it and flattens the lens for distance. The zonule itself has no nerve or vessel supply.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none (avascular; nourished by aqueous and vitreous). Venous: none. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Zonular weakness causes ectopia lentis: upward and outward (superotemporal) in Marfan syndrome (FBN1), downward in homocystinuria; also in pseudoexfoliation, trauma and Weill-Marchesani syndrome. Phacodonesis (trembling lens) and iridodonesis are signs. A capsular tension ring supports weak zonules in cataract surgery. Loss of accommodation with age is presbyopia.

### right-vitreous-body
- latinName: Corpus vitreum
- summary: A transparent gel of about 99 percent water with hyaluronan and type II collagen fibrils, about 4 ml and about four fifths of the eye's volume. It adheres most firmly at the vitreous base, the optic disc margin, the macula and along retinal vessels; the hyaloid canal is a remnant of the fetal hyaloid artery.
- location: Behind the lens (patellar fossa) and filling the vitreous cavity of the right eye against the retina and ciliary body.
- function: Maintains the globe's shape, transmits light, buffers shocks and holds the retina against the choroid.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none (avascular; nourished from surrounding tissue). Venous: none. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Age-related posterior vitreous detachment causes floaters and flashes and, if traction tears the retina, retinal tears and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (a curtain over vision). Vitreous haemorrhage follows proliferative diabetic retinopathy, tears or trauma. Asteroid hyalosis, vitritis, persistent fetal vasculature and endophthalmitis occur; vitrectomy treats several of them.

### right-cornea
- latinName: Cornea
- summary: The transparent, avascular front of the globe, about 0.5 mm thick centrally and 11.5 mm across, with five layers: epithelium (non-keratinised, 5 to 6 cells), Bowman layer, stroma (about 90 percent, regularly arranged collagen and keratocytes), Descemet membrane and endothelium. It derives from surface ectoderm (epithelium) and neural crest (stroma and endothelium).
- location: The anterior sixth of the right globe, continuous with the sclera at the limbus (where limbal stem cells lie), in front of the anterior chamber and iris.
- function: Provides about two thirds of the eye's refractive power (about 43 dioptres) and protects the interior. Clarity depends on the pumping endothelium. Densely innervated by the ophthalmic nerve (V1) through the long ciliary nerves; the corneal reflex has an afferent limb in V1 and an efferent limb in the facial nerve.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none; nourished by tears, aqueous humour and limbal arcades. Venous: none. Lymph: none, which together with the absence of vessels gives immune privilege and favours graft survival.
- clinicalNotes: Corneal abrasion (stains with fluorescein), herpes simplex keratitis (dendritic ulcer), bacterial keratitis (contact lens wearers), keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, arcus senilis and Kayser-Fleischer rings (Wilson disease) are seen. Neurotrophic keratopathy follows V1 damage. Limbal stem cell deficiency follows chemical burns. Keratoplasty (transplantation) and laser refractive surgery act here.

### right-iris
- latinName: Iris
- summary: The pigmented, contractile diaphragm of the uvea, with an anterior border layer, a vascular stroma (melanocytes decide the colour), a sphincter pupillae (circular smooth muscle) and a dilator pupillae (radial myoepithelium), and a posterior pigmented epithelium. The epithelium and muscles come from optic cup neuroectoderm and the stroma from neural crest.
- location: In front of the lens and behind the cornea of the right eye, dividing the anterior chamber from the posterior chamber; its root joins the ciliary body and its pupillary margin rests on the lens.
- function: Controls pupil size (about 2 to 4 mm in light, up to 8 mm in the dark). Sphincter: parasympathetic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus through the oculomotor nerve, ciliary ganglion and short ciliary nerves. Dilator: sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion through the long ciliary nerves.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the major arterial circle of the iris, formed by the long posterior ciliary and anterior ciliary arteries. Venous: vortex veins. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Anterior uveitis (iritis) causes pain, photophobia, ciliary flush, a small irregular pupil and posterior synechiae. A small pupil with ptosis is Horner syndrome, and a fixed dilated pupil is third nerve palsy or pharmacological dilation; an afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn) shows optic nerve or severe retinal disease. Angle-closure glaucoma involves iris bombe and is treated by laser peripheral iridotomy. Hyphaema, aniridia (PAX6) and iris melanoma are further conditions; tamsulosin causes intraoperative floppy iris syndrome.

### right-lens
- latinName: Lens
- summary: A transparent, avascular, biconvex structure about 9 to 10 mm across and 4 mm thick (thickening with age), made of an elastic capsule (type IV collagen), an anterior epithelium and tightly packed fibres rich in crystallins. It grows throughout life and develops from surface ectoderm (lens placode and vesicle).
- location: Behind the iris and pupil, in front of the vitreous (hyaloid fossa) of the right eye, held by the zonule.
- function: Provides about 15 to 20 dioptres of adjustable focusing power (accommodation) and filters ultraviolet light. Metabolism is largely anaerobic with nutrition from aqueous humour. It has no innervation; accommodation is driven by the ciliary muscle (parasympathetic, oculomotor nerve).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: none; the embryonic hyaloid artery regresses. Venous: none. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Cataract (nuclear sclerosis with age, cortical, posterior subcapsular with steroids; snowflake cataract in diabetes; congenital: rubella, galactosaemia) is treated by phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation. Presbyopia is loss of accommodation. Ectopia lentis and diabetic lens swelling (transient myopia) occur. A white pupil (leukocoria) in a child may be cataract or retinoblastoma, so the red reflex is checked at birth.

### right-levator-palpebrae-superioris
- latinName: Musculus levator palpebrae superioris
- summary: A thin skeletal muscle that raises the upper eyelid; its aponeurosis inserts into the anterior surface of the superior tarsal plate and the skin (forming the lid crease), with the smooth superior tarsal (Muller) muscle beneath it. The superior transverse (Whitnall) ligament acts as a pulley.
- location: From the lesser wing of the sphenoid above the optic canal, running forward along the roof of the right orbit above the superior rectus to the upper lid.
- function: Raises the upper lid (about 10 mm of excursion); Muller muscle adds about 2 mm. Innervation: the superior division of the oculomotor nerve (levator), and sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion (Muller muscle).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: muscular, lacrimal and supraorbital branches of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: superior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: eyelid lymphatics to preauricular and submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Ptosis is caused by third nerve palsy (severe, complete), Horner syndrome (mild, with miosis and anhidrosis), myasthenia gravis (fatigable, improved by an ice test) and aponeurotic dehiscence (age, contact lens use); congenital ptosis shows poor levator function. Thyroid eye disease causes lid retraction. Surgery includes levator advancement and frontalis sling.

### right-superior-oblique
- latinName: Musculus obliquus superior
- summary: The extraocular muscle with a fibrocartilaginous pulley (trochlea): the muscle arises from the sphenoid body above the optic canal, its tendon passes through the trochlea at the superomedial orbital rim and turns back to insert on the upper outer posterior part of the globe.
- location: Along the superomedial wall of the right orbit, with the tendon reflected under the superior rectus.
- function: Intorts the eye, depresses it when it is turned inward and abducts it slightly. It is supplied by the trochlear nerve (CN IV), the only cranial nerve that leaves the dorsal brainstem; its fibres cross before exiting, so each superior oblique is supplied by the opposite trochlear nucleus.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the lateral muscular branch of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: superior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Trochlear nerve palsy causes vertical or oblique diplopia, worse on downgaze (reading, stairs), with a head tilt away from the affected side and hypertropia of the affected eye. The nerve has the longest intracranial course and is a common site of closed head injury damage; other causes are microvascular disease and congenital palsy. Brown syndrome involves the tendon sheath.

### right-inferior-rectus
- latinName: Musculus rectus inferior
- summary: An extraocular muscle arising from the common tendinous ring (annulus of Zinn) and inserting on the lower front of the sclera about 6.5 mm behind the limbus.
- location: Along the floor of the right orbit, above the orbital floor and the infraorbital groove, in contact with inferior oblique.
- function: Depresses the eye (strongest when abducted), with extorsion and slight adduction. Innervation: the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: lateral muscular branch of the ophthalmic artery and the infraorbital artery. Venous: inferior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: An orbital floor (blow-out) fracture can trap it or surrounding tissue, causing limited upgaze, vertical diplopia, infraorbital numbness and enophthalmos; the oculocardiac reflex (bradycardia, nausea) is typical in children with trapdoor fractures, who need urgent surgery. It is the muscle most often involved in thyroid eye disease, restricting upgaze. A forced duction test distinguishes restriction from palsy.

### right-lateral-rectus
- latinName: Musculus rectus lateralis
- summary: An extraocular muscle arising from the common tendinous ring and inserting about 7 mm behind the limbus on the lateral sclera.
- location: Along the lateral wall of the right orbit, with the lacrimal gland above and the lateral palpebral ligament in front.
- function: Abducts the eye. Innervation: the abducens nerve (CN VI), part of the mnemonic LR6 SO4.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the lacrimal artery and lateral muscular branch of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: superior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Abducens palsy causes horizontal diplopia, worse at distance and on looking toward the affected side, with esotropia. The nerve's long intracranial course over the petrous apex (Dorello canal) makes it a false-localising sign of raised intracranial pressure; other causes are Gradenigo syndrome (petrositis), cavernous sinus lesions, pontine stroke and microvascular disease. Duane syndrome is a congenital co-contraction.

### right-superior-rectus
- latinName: Musculus rectus superior
- summary: An extraocular muscle arising from the common tendinous ring and inserting on the upper front of the sclera about 7.7 mm behind the limbus.
- location: Along the roof of the right orbit, beneath the levator palpebrae superioris.
- function: Elevates the eye (strongest when abducted), with intorsion and slight adduction. Innervation: the superior division of the oculomotor nerve, which also supplies the levator.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: medial and lateral muscular branches of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: superior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Oculomotor palsy limits upgaze; double elevator palsy and orbital roof fractures also impair it. In thyroid eye disease it is involved less often than the inferior and medial recti. Bell phenomenon (upward rolling of the eye on lid closure) protects the cornea. A superior rectus bridle suture is used in cataract surgery.

### right-medial-rectus
- latinName: Musculus rectus medialis
- summary: The largest extraocular muscle, arising from the common tendinous ring and inserting about 5.5 mm behind the limbus, the closest rectus to the limbus.
- location: Along the medial wall of the right orbit, next to the ethmoidal air cells separated by the lamina papyracea.
- function: Adducts the eye; both medial recti work together to converge the eyes. Innervation: the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the medial muscular branch of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: superior and inferior ophthalmic veins. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Oculomotor palsy leaves the eye looking down and out. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion; multiple sclerosis in the young, brainstem infarct in the elderly) causes failure of adduction on lateral gaze with nystagmus in the abducting eye. Thyroid eye disease affects the medial rectus often (order: inferior, medial, superior, lateral). Ethmoid sinusitis causes orbital cellulitis through the lamina papyracea; esotropia surgery recesses this muscle.

### right-inferior-oblique
- latinName: Musculus obliquus inferior
- summary: The only extraocular muscle that does not arise at the orbital apex: it arises from the orbital floor (maxilla) near the lacrimal groove, passes below the inferior rectus and inserts on the posterolateral sclera.
- location: In the anterior floor of the right orbit, beneath the inferior rectus and above the orbital floor, close to the nasolacrimal canal.
- function: Extorts the eye and elevates it when turned inward, with slight abduction. Innervation: the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve, which also carries the parasympathetic root to the ciliary ganglion.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the infraorbital artery and the medial muscular branch of the ophthalmic artery. Venous: inferior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: none.
- clinicalNotes: Overaction follows congenital superior oblique palsy (a V-pattern). It can be entrapped in orbital floor fractures. Oculomotor palsy affects it; surgical weakening (recession, myectomy) corrects overaction.

### right-lacrimal-gland
- latinName: Glandula lacrimalis
- summary: A serous tubuloacinar gland with a larger orbital lobe and a smaller palpebral lobe divided by the levator aponeurosis; 10 to 12 ducts open into the lateral superior conjunctival fornix. Accessory glands (of Krause and Wolfring) add basal tearing. It derives from surface ectoderm buds of the conjunctival epithelium.
- location: In the lacrimal fossa of the frontal bone in the superolateral part of the right orbit.
- function: Secretes the aqueous layer of tears (reflex tearing: afferent V1 from the cornea; efferent parasympathetic fibres from the greater petrosal nerve of VII to the pterygopalatine ganglion, then the zygomatic and lacrimal nerves). Sympathetic fibres follow the deep petrosal nerve.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the lacrimal artery (ophthalmic). Venous: superior ophthalmic vein. Lymph: preauricular (parotid) nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca; Schirmer test) is common in Sjogren syndrome. Dacryoadenitis occurs in mumps, EBV, sarcoidosis and IgG4-related disease. Tumours include pleomorphic adenoma (benign) and adenoid cystic carcinoma (malignant). Crocodile tears follow misdirected facial nerve regeneration.

### right-lacrimal-canaliculus
- latinName: Canaliculus lacrimalis
- summary: A tiny tube in each eyelid: a vertical segment (about 2 mm) from the punctum on the lacrimal papilla, then a horizontal segment (about 8 mm); the two usually unite as a common canaliculus entering the lacrimal sac. It is lined by stratified squamous epithelium and surrounded by the lacrimal part of the orbicularis (Horner muscle).
- location: At the medial margin of the upper and lower lids of the right eye, running toward the lacrimal sac behind the medial canthal tendon.
- function: Drains tears by capillary action and by the pumping action of blinking (the lower canaliculus carries most). Motor: facial nerve to orbicularis; sensory: infratrochlear nerve (V1).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: angular artery and the palpebral arteries. Venous: angular vein. Lymph: submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Lacerations of the medial lower lid can cut the canaliculus and are repaired over a stent to prevent chronic tearing. Punctal stenosis and canaliculitis (Actinomyces; a pouting punctum with discharge) cause epiphora. Punctal plugs are used for dry eye.

### right-nasolacrimal-duct
- latinName: Ductus nasolacrimalis
- summary: A membranous duct about 12 to 18 mm long running in the bony nasolacrimal canal (maxilla, lacrimal bone and inferior turbinate), lined by columnar epithelium with goblet cells; it opens under the inferior turbinate through the valve of Hasner. It develops from a solid ectodermal cord that canalises late in fetal life.
- location: From the inferior end of the lacrimal sac on the right side, along the lateral wall of the nose to the inferior meatus.
- function: Drains tears into the nose, which is why the nose runs when crying.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: angular and infraorbital arteries and nasal branches of the sphenopalatine artery. Venous: angular and nasal veins. Lymph: submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction (5 to 20 percent of newborns; failure of the Hasner valve to open) causes watering and discharge and resolves by 12 months in most; probing is used if it persists. Acquired obstruction follows age, sinus disease or trauma; dacryocystorhinostomy creates a bypass. Tear drainage is checked with the dye disappearance test.

### right-lacrimal-sac
- latinName: Saccus lacrimalis
- summary: A membranous sac about 12 mm long, lined by columnar epithelium, continuous below with the nasolacrimal duct and receiving the common canaliculus above.
- location: In the lacrimal fossa between the anterior and posterior lacrimal crests (maxilla and lacrimal bone) at the medial canthus of the right eye; the medial canthal tendon crosses its anterior surface, with most of the sac below the tendon.
- function: Collects tears from the canaliculi and passes them to the nasolacrimal duct; the lacrimal part of the orbicularis compresses it during blinking.
- bloodSupply: Arterial: angular, superior and inferior palpebral and infraorbital arteries. Venous: angular vein. Lymph: submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Obstruction leads to dacryocystitis (acute painful red swelling below the medial canthal tendon, usually Staphylococcus aureus) or chronic mucocele; swelling above the tendon suggests a frontoethmoidal lesion instead. Dacryocystorhinostomy treats chronic obstruction, and tumours of the sac are rare.

### right-lacrimal-lake
- latinName: Lacus lacrimalis
- summary: The small triangular space at the medial canthus of the eye in which tears collect, containing the lacrimal caruncle (a fleshy island with sebaceous glands and fine hairs), the plica semilunaris and the lacrimal puncta on their papillae.
- location: At the medial angle of the right palpebral fissure, between the eyelid margins and the medial canthus.
- function: Collects tears before they drain through the puncta into the canaliculi. Sensory supply: infratrochlear nerve (V1).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: angular artery and medial palpebral arteries. Venous: angular vein. Lymph: submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Epiphora (overflow of tears) occurs with drainage obstruction or lid malposition such as ectropion. Basal cell carcinoma of the medial canthus tends to invade the orbit and lacrimal drainage. Telecanthus follows naso-orbito-ethmoid fractures, and naevi and papillomas arise on the caruncle.

### tarsal-plate-of-right-upper-eyelid
- latinName: Tarsus superior palpebrae
- summary: A plate of dense fibrous connective tissue (not cartilage), about 25 to 30 mm long and 10 mm high in the centre, containing 25 to 40 meibomian (tarsal) glands, which are holocrine sebaceous glands opening on the lid margin behind the lashes.
- location: In the upper eyelid of the right eye between the orbicularis (in front) and the palpebral conjunctiva (behind), attached to the orbital rim by the medial and lateral canthal tendons; the levator aponeurosis inserts on its front and the superior tarsal muscle on its upper border.
- function: Gives the upper lid shape and stiffness; meibomian secretions form the oily layer of the tear film, which slows tear evaporation. Sensory supply: supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves (V1).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the marginal and peripheral arterial arcades of the upper lid (medial and lateral palpebral arteries, from the ophthalmic and facial/superficial temporal arteries). Venous: superior ophthalmic and facial veins. Lymph: lateral two thirds to preauricular nodes, medial third to submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: A chalazion is a painless granuloma from a blocked meibomian gland; a hordeolum (stye) is an acute staphylococcal infection of lash or tarsal glands. Meibomian gland dysfunction and blepharitis cause evaporative dry eye. Trachoma scars the tarsal conjunctiva and causes trichiasis; eversion of the upper lid reveals follicles or subtarsal foreign bodies. Sebaceous carcinoma can masquerade as a recurrent chalazion.

### tarsal-plate-of-right-lower-eyelid
- latinName: Tarsus inferior palpebrae
- summary: A smaller plate of dense fibrous tissue, about 20 mm long and 5 mm high, containing 20 to 30 meibomian glands; the capsulopalpebral fascia (lower lid retractors) attaches to its lower border.
- location: In the lower eyelid of the right eye between the orbicularis and the conjunctiva, anchored by the medial and lateral canthal tendons.
- function: Supports the lower lid and houses oil-producing glands that stabilise the tear film. Sensory supply: infraorbital nerve (V2).
- bloodSupply: Arterial: the marginal arcade of the lower lid and branches of the infraorbital and angular arteries. Venous: facial and ophthalmic veins. Lymph: lateral part to preauricular nodes, medial part to submandibular nodes.
- clinicalNotes: Involutional laxity of the tarsus, canthal tendons or retractors causes ectropion (lid turned out, with epiphora) or entropion (lid turned in, with corneal irritation). Chalazion, hordeolum and meibomian gland dysfunction occur. The lower lid is the commonest site of eyelid basal cell carcinoma, and lower lid retraction can follow blepharoplasty or thyroid eye disease.
